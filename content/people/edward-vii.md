A long list of clothes is credited to him, and the credits are hard to pin down. He is linked to a hat, a check, a trouser hem, an evening jacket and a waistcoat habit. Some of this is documented, some is a tailor's account of its best customer, and some is a story.

## His tailor

[[henry-poole|Henry Poole & Co]] says it became his chief tailor after his first visit in 1860, and quotes the [[duke-of-windsor|Duke of Windsor]] on it. The firm lists his innovations as the Prince of Wales check, the Homburg, the [[norfolk-jacket|Norfolk jacket]] and black tie worn with evening tails. These are the maker's own claims.

## The dinner jacket

Poole says it cut him a prototype smoking jacket in blue silk in 1865, for private dinners at [[sandringham|Sandringham]], as an informal alternative to the white tie tailcoat. Favourbrook tells the same story with the same year and says Poole's records describe a "celestial-blue smoking jacket with matching trousers". Wikipedia gives 1860 instead, and says only that Poole is "widely believed" to have made the first modern [[dinner-jacket|dinner jacket]]. It adds that James Potter visited Sandringham in 1886, was told by the Prince to have one made at Poole, and wore it at the Tuxedo Club, which gave the American name. Favourbrook is more cautious about the Potter link.

## Hat, check and trousers

The Met says that as Prince of Wales he popularised the [[homburg|Homburg]] in England after bringing it back from regular trips to the spa at Homburg in Germany. Wikipedia agrees, dating it to the 1890s after a visit to Bad Homburg. Wikipedia says the [[glen-check|Glen Urquhart check]] was first used in the 1840s by the Countess of Seafield to dress gamekeepers, and that he first made it famous; Edward VIII popularised it later.

Parisian Gentleman says he purportedly pioneered the [[trouser-turn-up|turn-up]] in the 1890s, tailored in with the back shorter than the front, though photographs are inconclusive. Gentleman's Gazette gives 1890. A 1922 *Chicago Tribune* report quoted by Put This On says "the late King Edward" sometimes wore side-creased trousers.

## The bottom button

Mental Floss says, without a source, that he grew too stout to fasten the bottom button of his [[waistcoat]] and kept it open because he liked the look. Poole also ties the open button to the stoutness behind his nickname "Tum-Tum". Wikipedia, citing a 2012 *GQ* piece by Robert Johnston, calls the explanation a myth. Treat it as a story.
