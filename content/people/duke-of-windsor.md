When the Prince of Wales toured the United States in 1924, the trade paper *Men's Wear* remarked that young American men cared more about his clothes than anyone else's. The remark reaches us second-hand, through Wikipedia citing Alan Flusser's *Dressing the Man* (2002). Wikipedia says he set men's fashion at the height of his popularity, and the V&A calls him a leader of men's fashion, known for a casual style and bold colours, patterns and textures.

He was born at White Lodge in Richmond Park on 23 June 1894, became Prince of Wales in 1910 and was king from January to December 1936. After he abdicated he was the Duke of Windsor, and he died in Paris in 1972.

## His tailors

Wikipedia says [[frederick-scholte|Frederick Scholte]], an Amsterdam-born [[savile-row]] tailor, was his principal tailor from 1919 until Scholte died in 1948; some sources give 1959, which Wikipedia calls a mistake. According to Wikipedia, Scholte invented the [[drape-cut|drape]] suit for him, introduced in the 1930s: wide shoulders, roomy armholes and a narrow waist borrowed from Household Cavalry uniform, against the tighter Victorian cut.

The Met holds one of his evening suits, designed by Scholte. When the Duke was Governor of the Bahamas in the Second World War, H. Harris, a New York tailor trained in London, made duplicate trousers for it, an arrangement the Duke called an "international compromise". The Met also credits him with bringing midnight blue [[evening-dress|evening wear]] in the 1920s as a softer alternative to black.

## Checks and golf

The V&A has a 1940 single-breasted lounge suit of his in mustard wool [[glen-check|Glen Urquhart or Prince of Wales check]], made by Trimingham. He gave it to Cecil Beaton for the 1971 exhibition *Fashion: An Anthology*. For how that check got its royal name, see [[english-country]].

A 1922 press photograph in the Royal Collection shows him as captain of the golf club at St Andrews, driving off in [[plus-fours]]. A V&A Dundee blog says a 1920s [[fair-isle|Fair Isle]] jumper design was famously worn by a Prince of Wales at golf, which helped the knitters of Shetland and Fair Isle; it gives no date.

## The knot

His name is on the [[windsor-knot]], but by his own account he neither invented nor tied it. He tied a simple four-in-hand and got a fuller knot from broad, thickly interlined ties made for him in the 1920s.
