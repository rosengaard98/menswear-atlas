Nobody agrees when the heritage revival began, or even what to call it. Writers have named it the heritage boom, the workwear boom, the lumberjack look, neo-trad and Take Ivy 2.0, and each gives different dates. This entry starts it in 2004 because that is the earliest year a source gives.

## Where it came from

W. David Marx, in *The Business of Fashion*, says [[thom-browne|Thom Browne]] began a re-evaluation of American trad in 2004 with a charcoal grey suit, white oxford shirt and rep tie. The wider industry then added British suits, tweeds, plaids and rugged [[workwear]]. In Japan, he says, *Brutus* and *Popeye* promoted Anglo-American neo-trad for about two years, and select shops such as [[beams-plus|Beams Plus]] sold the heritage look to steady crowds.

Marx's larger argument, made in his 2015 book *Ametora*, is that Japan kept American clothing alive while Americans lost interest. In a Put This On interview he says Big John and Studio D'Artisan kept suppliers making [[selvedge-denim|selvedge denim]] after American mills dropped narrow shuttle looms in the early 1980s, and that Japanese demand for makers such as Alden kept heritage firms stable. Americans, he says, then went back to Japanese examples of American style, the [[ametora]] that had kept the [[ivy-league-look|Ivy look]] going. That is his thesis, not a measured fact.

Other writers start elsewhere. [[self-edge|Self Edge]], a shop importing Japanese denim labels such as Iron Heart and Flat Head, opened in 2006 and added a Los Angeles branch on 1 September 2010. Victor Basa, in a 2011 Philippine lifestyle column, traced the plaid flannel, straight jeans and [[red-wing|Red Wing]] boots of the lumberjack look to a 2006 "Mountain Man" look, and to hard times and nostalgia. Charles Mcfarlane says the movement grew out of the 2009 recession. *[[take-ivy|Take Ivy]]*, first published in Japan in 1965, came out in English on 31 August 2010; only InsideHook's "Take Ivy 2.0" framing ties it to the trend.

## Mainstream and decline

Mcfarlane, a former shop employee, recalls [[j-crew|J.Crew]]'s Men's Shop and the vintage-inspired Wallace & Barnes line, mixing workwear and suiting with raw denim, and says the movement was in its twilight by 2012. Ryan Chang adds [[ralph-lauren|Ralph Lauren]]'s RRL and says heritage had been dead for a few years by 2020. He says J.Crew's upmarket offerings took the look beyond specialists, and lists shifts that came before it faded: Japanese brands leaving period-correct looks, shorter attention spans and the rise of streetwear. Marx's undated piece said only that the boom might be peaking, as beards, [[barbour|Barbour]] coats and Indy boots lost their novelty. FashionBeans recalls forums making straight-cut selvedge an early-2010s obsession, set against skinny jeans.
