In 1766 Madrid rioted over a hat and a cape. The long capa and broad-brimmed hat were part of the city's popular dress, and the attempt to ban them is the best-known episode in the majos' story.

## Who the majos were

The Making of Madrid describes majos as working-class Madrileños from poorer districts such as Maravillas, now Malasaña, and Lavapiés, proud of being Spanish, with bullfighting among their pursuits. The V&A's catalogue note tells it differently: majos were immigrants from other regions who brought their regional dress to 18th-century Madrid. The V&A adds that Madrid's elite adopted the dress as a patriotic Spanish style, against French fashion.

## The clothes

Ana María Velasco Molpeceres lists the man's outfit: calzón, or breeches, with stockings, a short jaquetilla or a jubón, a faja or sash at the waist, and natural hair under a redecilla, a hairnet, topped by a montera or small hat. Over it went the long [[capa]] and the very wide [[chambergo]], which let the wearer muffle his face. She says the chambergo had little Spanish tradition and arrived with General Schömberg's troops. In [[goya|Goya]]'s tapestry cartoon *The Parasol* (c. 1777, Prado) the young man is a majo, hair in a net, with a colourful silk belt.

## Cape and hat

The crown issued edicts against the long cape in 1716, 1719, 1723, 1729, 1737, 1740 and 1745, all without effect. In 1766 the minister Esquilache's bando required the short cape and three-cornered hat, on the grounds that the old ones hid weapons and faces. Wikipedia and Velasco Molpeceres date it 10 March; Sergio Cañas gives 20 March. The [[esquilache-riots|riots]] began on 23 March, lasted three days and left about forty dead, and Esquilache was removed. Wikipedia and Cañas say the reform went on, with the Count of Aranda reportedly persuading the guilds that the chambergo and long cape were the hangman's dress. The Making of Madrid says the decree was withdrawn.

## Majismo

Aristocrats then dressed as majos and [[maja|majas]]. Velasco Molpeceres says this mattered most under Carlos IV, was gone by the reign of Isabel II, and points to Goya's portraits of María Luisa de Parma and the [[duchess-of-alba|Duchess of Alba]]. Rich children dressed as majos for the San Isidro pilgrimage. The V&A holds a pink silk satin majos suit of 1800-1825, braided and fringed in silver, worn as fancy dress in the 19th century.

The bullfighter's [[traje-de-luces|traje de luces]] also has 18th-century roots in the showy dress around the bullfight, and the Phoenix Art Museum says it drew on the dress of Spain's lower classes. None of these sources names majo dress as its origin; any direct line between the two is an inference.
