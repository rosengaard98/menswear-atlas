The look took shape on elite American campuses from the 1920s to the 1940s. By 1915 the [[ocbd]] was already worn at almost every East Coast college, and in the decades that followed students assembled a whole wardrobe around it. In the 1950s it left campus and became what middle-class American men wore: in 1957 and 1958 roughly seven out of ten suits sold in the country were cut in the Ivy style.

## The pieces

- The [[sack-suit]]: single-breasted, a three-roll-two front, no darts, a single back vent and a soft, boxy cut.
- Trousers with a lower rise, worn with a belt, usually without pleats or turn-ups.
- Oxford button-downs instead of starched dress shirts.
- [[penny-loafer|Penny loafers]] or brogues instead of oxford shoes.
- Khaki [[chinos]], which veterans studying on the G.I. Bill brought to campus in the late 1940s.
- Knitted silk ties, sweaters and cardigans, argyle socks and tweed sport coats.

[[brooks-brothers]] and [[j-press|J. Press]] were the main places to buy it.

## Afterwards

By the end of the 1960s young men had moved on to the wide lapels, flared trousers and bright colours of the [[peacock-revolution]] and to hippie clothing. The style survived in two places. In Japan, [[take-ivy]] (1965) became the reference book for a generation of fans. In America it came back in the early 1980s, brighter and more casual, as [[preppy]].
