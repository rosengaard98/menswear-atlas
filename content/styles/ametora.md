Ametora is Japanese slang for "American traditional". The writer W. David Marx uses it for the whole story of Japan and American clothes, from Ivy through denim, outdoor wear and streetwear, and argues that Japan kept Ivy knowledge alive while the look nearly vanished in the United States. This entry covers the first phase, Japanese Ivy. Four of its seven sources are Marx's own writing or interviews with him.

## VAN and the Ivy model

[[kensuke-ishizu|Kensuke Ishizu]] was born in Okayama in 1911 and worked in Tianjin before the war. Afterwards he met American soldiers, a Princeton man among them. In 1951 he founded [[van-jacket|VAN Jacket]] in Osaka; Ivy Style says the name came from the left-wing tabloid *Vanguard*, *Lapham's Quarterly* says from a comic book title. VAN began with British-style suits and from the late 1950s made a full East Coast college line. After visiting Princeton in 1959, Ishizu launched an "Ivy model" suit that *Lapham's* says copied [[brooks-brothers|Brooks Brothers]]' Number One [[sack-suit]]. He died in 2005, aged 94 by Ivy Style's count and 93 by *Lapham's*.

Who turned VAN to Ivy is disputed. [[toshiyuki-kurosu|Toshiyuki Kurosu]], who joined in 1961 aged 24, says he persuaded Ishizu; Marx credits the 1959 trip. Kurosu recalls Japanese tailors adding a third button to a two-button jacket because they did not understand the cut. With the illustrator [[kazuo-hozumi|Kazuo Hozumi]] he founded the Traditional Ivy Leaguers Club; Kurosu says he was one of the people who made its rules. In 1963 Ishizu introduced TPO, dressing for time, place and occasion. Magazines such as [[mens-club|*Men's Club*]] carried the message: Kurosu says VAN's T-shirts sold only after the magazine declared that all American students wore them.

## The Miyuki-zoku

In summer 1964 young people in Ivy clothes gathered on Miyuki Street in Ginza. They wore [[ocbd|button-down oxfords]], [[madras]], slim cropped khaki or white trousers, and [[penny-loafer|penny loafers]], changing out of school uniform in café bathrooms and carrying it in brown paper bags. Put This On, quoting Marx, puts the crowd at about 700 at its peak; *Lapham's* says about 2,000 each weekend. Parents and PTAs pushed for bans. Kurosu says the crowd hurt Ivy's image, which is why [[take-ivy|*Take Ivy*]] (1965) set out to show real students.

## Fall and return

VAN filed for reorganisation on 6 April 1978 and went bankrupt again in 1984. Itochu took the name in 2000: Wikipedia says it bought it, Ivy Style that it licensed it. The American [[preppy]] trend of the early 1980s, with a Japanese edition of *The Official Preppy Handbook*, brought Ivy back in Japan.
