J. Press sells more in Japan than at home: its Japanese business is reported to be about six times the American one. Yet the firm began as a tailor to [[yale-university|Yale]] men in New Haven, and the family and the firm date it to 1902.

## New Haven

The founder was Jacobi Press, an immigrant tailor who had meant to become a rabbi. The accounts of his start differ. Wikipedia and the firm say he came from Latvia and founded J. Press in 1902. His grandson Richard Press, writing in Ivy Style, says he came from the Pale of Russia, learned tailoring and English as an intern at J.C. Goldbaum's shop on Chapel Street, and bought that business in 1902. It traded as Goldbaum & Press, later J. Press, and in 1907 Jacobi bought the building at 262 York Street, opposite Pierson College.

A New York store followed in 1912, placed between the Yale and Harvard Clubs, according to Wikipedia. Irving E. Press, Jacobi's son, led the firm in its best years, and the Shaggy Dog Shetland sweater, the [[shaggy-dog-sweater]], is credited to that time.

## The house look

Wikipedia lists the signatures: Shaggy Dog sweaters, Indian [[madras]] jackets, the [[ocbd|Oxford button-down]], [[repp-tie|repp ties]], three-button [[sack-suit|sack jackets]] and plain-front trousers. The New Haven Independent describes the jacket as a straight-falling sack with unpadded shoulders and three buttons, the top one left undone, worn with a regimental tie about 3 1/4 inches wide. In 2009 a Cambridge store manager told Ivy Style that J. Press made the "closest thing to any natural shouldered garment in the industry", a claim for the [[natural-shoulder]]. He also put J. Press and [[brooks-brothers|Brooks Brothers]] on the same baseline in the Ivy years.

The firm says it is widely credited with creating the [[ivy-league-look]]. That is the maker's claim; the same Ivy Style interview puts Brooks on the same baseline. Client lists are house marketing too: Wikipedia names [[duke-ellington|Duke Ellington]], [[cary-grant|Cary Grant]] and Robert Frost.

## Japan

J. Press licensed its name in Japan in 1974, said to be the first American brand to do so. The licensee, [[onward-kashiyama|Onward Kashiyama]], bought the company in 1986, ending family control. The firm says it was prominent in [[take-ivy|*Take Ivy*]] (1965) and in Tokyo's Ivy boom, part of the story of [[ametora]]. It also links its fame to the [[preppy]] wave. When the Museum at FIT showed its Ivy Style exhibition in Tokyo in October and November 2013, J. Press ran a pop-up in Isetan Shinjuku.

The New Haven store was badly damaged by a storm in February 2013. A new store at 262 Elm Street opened around the 120th anniversary, with Richard Press, then 84, as consultant and company historian.
