*Dress for Success* treated a man's suit as a tool for getting ahead and claimed to measure what each garment did for him. [[john-t-molloy|John T. Molloy]]'s book was first published in the United States in 1975 by Peter H. Wyden and became a bestseller, though none of the sources here gives sales figures.

## The method

Molloy called himself a "wardrobe engineer" and, according to Tina Sutton in *The Boston Globe*, said the men's book rested on 15 years of research. As the sources describe it, he showed people drawings or photographs of outfits and recorded what they thought of the wearer, such as whether he looked competent or fitted in with other employees. He tested reactions to single garments, such as a beige against a black raincoat, and surveyed what men wore. Wikipedia quotes Molloy's own claim, made in the women's book, that his work was based on scientific research. Joanne Entwistle, in the *Encyclopedia of Clothing and Fashion*, treats it as a pseudo-science of clothing built on quantitative testing. Alan Pell Crawford says Molloy insisted it rested on empirical research, and compares it himself to the later focus group. Alison Lurie, reviewing the women's book in 1978, ran her own informal polls and found they backed him.

## The advice

Entwistle sums up the rules for men as black or grey suits, ties that are not too daring, and polished shoes. Crawford says Molloy thought a business suit a safe choice if one's superiors wore one, and that the point was to conform to conservative, class-conscious dress; critics called it an executive herd instinct, and Molloy agreed. There were exceptions. Creative men on Madison Avenue could wear a sport coat with a [[pocket-square|pocket square]] and loafers instead of [[brogue|wingtips]]. A man from outside the South should not wear a polka-dot tie to sell there, and Sutton says letting a wife or girlfriend choose one's clothes ranked as the worst mistake of all.

In a 1988 interview with *The Christian Science Monitor* about the revised *New Dress for Success*, Molloy said bow ties read as an outsider's, a red tie looks honest when it is nearer maroon than bright red, and a beige suit with a grey tinge works best across the country. He claimed contrasting-collar shirts had risen from 1 per cent of men to over 60 per cent.
## Afterwards

*The Woman's Dress for Success Book* followed in 1977; Entwistle gives both 1979 and 1980 in her entry. Wikipedia and Entwistle say the two books popularised [[power-dressing|power dressing]] and helped start the 1980s power suit in grey, blue or navy with [[shoulder-pads|shoulder pads]]. Entwistle sets them alongside an image-consultancy industry and says the backlash came with [[dress-down-friday|dress-down Fridays]] in the 1990s. A bank executive told Lurie that the advantage disappears once everyone dresses alike.
