The press called it the Peacock Revolution: a 1960s return of dandyism to men's dress, strongest in England and centred on London. Wikipedia credits the consumer psychologist Ernest Dichter with the term in 1965 and George Frazier of *Esquire* with spreading it in his columns in 1968. *Day of the Peacock*, published with the V&A, covers 1963 to 1973; Wikipedia stretches the span from the late 1950s to the mid-1970s, with a peak around 1966 to 1969 and a second, glam-rock wave around 1972 to 1974. The atlas draws it from 1965, after the [[postwar]] years, to 1975.

## London

The V&A traces it to the [[mod|Mods]] of the late 1950s, who took up slim Italian suits and short jackets, and to [[john-stephen|John Stephen]], who opened his first [[carnaby-street|Carnaby Street]] boutique in 1957 selling cheap, sharp, colourful suits. The Beatles made collarless jackets popular in 1963. By the mid-1960s the modern dandy wore frills and velvet, once thought too feminine, and Mick Jagger's Victorian guardsman's jacket on *Ready Steady Go!* sped up a fashion for military uniform. The V&A names Hung On You and Granny Takes a Trip on the King's Road and Blades on Dover Street as the most influential boutiques.

[[michael-fish|Michael Fish]] learned shirtmaking at Turnbull & Asser, where he made [[kipper-tie|kipper ties]] by widening ties, and opened Mr Fish on Clifford Street in Mayfair: in 1966 according to the V&A and Culture Stories, in 1964 according to Wikipedia. Jagger wore a white Mr Fish smock at Hyde Park in 1969, and David Bowie a floral satin dress in 1971. The V&A holds a double-breasted corduroy Mr Fish suit made by Hexter around 1968.

[[savile-row|Savile Row]] changed too. New tailors settled in the streets around it, and Nutters of Savile Row, founded by [[tommy-nutter|Tommy Nutter]], opened at 35a in 1969, mixing daring fashion into bespoke. Three Beatles wore Nutters suits on the *Abbey Road* cover.

## America

Wikipedia says the look arrived around 1964 with the British Invasion. John Stephen licensed Carnaby Street shops in department stores, Sears opened mod shops, and Pierre Cardin's styles reached the US in 1966. Gentleman's Gazette lists coloured dinner jackets from After Six in 1965, ruffled shirts and velvet and brocade dinner jackets.

## The end

By the late 1960s, the V&A says, the Vietnam War and student uprisings in France turned some young people towards second-hand clothes and 1930s and 1940s vintage. In Wikipedia's account *Bonnie and Clyde* (1967) revived 1930s dress, which [[ralph-lauren|Ralph Lauren]] and Bill Blass built on, and John T. Molloy's [[dress-for-success|*Dress for Success*]] (1975) marked the return to conservative business dress. The same page argues the revolution's reach has been exaggerated and that many men never took it up.
