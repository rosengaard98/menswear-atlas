The frock coat dominated: usually dark wool, tight in the sleeve, with skirts reaching the knee. The morning coat, with its front edges curving away to the back, was the alternative. Trousers were slim, often pale or striped against the sober coat, and the trouser press made a sharp crease essential. Perfectly white, heavily starched linen was the sign of a well-turned-out man, and the high collar was tied with a silk scarf that slowly narrowed into the necktie. The [[detachable-collar]], said to have been invented in Troy, New York, in 1827 so that only the collar needed washing, grew into an industry that earned Troy the name Collar City.

Colour went into the waistcoat, in silk, brocade, paisley or tartan, often with pearl buttons and a watch chain across the front. The most popular hat of mid-century was the top hat in felted beaver, which in time gave way to the bowler.

## Signs of change

Over the century men's clothes grew less restrictive. Sport and leisure, boating among them, allowed brighter clothes, and a looser lounge jacket was worn indoors. The [[lounge-suit]] began as country and sporting wear, and professional men started wearing jackets and trousers that matched, the first business suits. In the 1890s the Prince of Wales, the future [[edward-vii|Edward VII]], popularised turn-ups on trousers.
