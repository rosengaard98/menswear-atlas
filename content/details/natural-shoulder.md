Ivy Style calls the natural shoulder the defining characteristic of the [[ivy-league-look|Ivy look]]. Its contributor James Moore describes it by its "lack of structural artifice" and sets it against structured shoulders: the military bearing of [[gieves-and-hawkes|Gieves & Hawkes]] or [[huntsman|Huntsman]], or the architectural roping of Cifonelli.

"Unpadded" is the word often used, and it is loose. A commenter on Ivy Style points out that a shoulder with no padding at all would hang like a shirt, and prefers "minimal padding". Bonne Gueule lists soft natural shoulders among the features of the [[sack-suit|sack suit]] and cites G. Bruce Boyer: the sack jacket has as little padding as a jacket can carry while still looking structured.

## Measuring it

Enthusiasts measure the shoulder seam point to point, from under the collar to the sleeve seam. Ivy Style gives 6 1/8 inches on a size 40, scaled for other sizes, and reports that [[j-press|J. Press]] shoulders were set by that figure for decades. A [[southwick|Southwick]] sport coat measured 6 inches; a Martin Greenfield jacket measured 6 3/8 and looked boxier. The author says the figure is not the whole story, and that tailoring counts too. Ivy Style also says the J. Press house tailor, Felix Samelson, altered shoulders to get the classic look, and that the factory technicians who made such jackets are gone. The Museum at FIT calls J. Press, founded in 1902 by Jacobi Press, "the best quality maker of the natural shoulder suit".

## Brooks Brothers and after

Ivy Style examined a mid-century [[brooks-brothers|Brooks Brothers]] two-button jacket and a No. 1 sack from the same owner. Both had minimal padding with a slight extension, a small, high [[armhole]] and a generously draped [[sleevehead]] that rippled like an Italian [[spalla-camicia|spalla camicia]]. The writer says Brooks built shoulders this way only for a brief period, and that by the late 1960s the natural shoulder had a smoother shape. Reader comments on another Ivy Style post name Southwick as the benchmark soft shoulder of the mid-to-late 1960s, with [[norman-hilton|Norman Hilton]] second.

The two terms overlap without being the same. Permanent Style says spalla camicia describes how the sleeve joins the shoulder, running under it as on a shirt, and need not mean a small shoulder or a gathered sleevehead.

None of these sources dates the natural shoulder's beginning or names an inventor.
