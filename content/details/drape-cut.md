A drape coat carries more cloth through the chest and shoulders than the body strictly needs, so it falls in a soft vertical fold that gives the cut its name. It is also called the London cut or the blade cut. Wikipedia describes it as a British variant of the three-piece suit developed in the 1930s.

## Who and when

The trade gives it to [[frederick-scholte|Frederick Scholte]] (1865 to 1948), an Amsterdam-born [[savile-row]] tailor who was principal tailor to the Prince of Wales, the future [[duke-of-windsor|Duke of Windsor]], from 1919 until his death. Wikipedia's Scholte page quotes a 1917 *Tailor & Cutter* review that warned against crediting any one man with a method, so treat this as the usual attribution.

The origin stories differ. Wikipedia's Scholte page says he was apprenticed to the Household Cavalry's tailors and took wide shoulders, roomy armholes and a narrow waist from their uniform. Put This On says he saw that belting a Guardsman's coat filled out the chest and nipped the waist. On dates, Wikipedia says the 1930s, while a 2021 *Rake* piece by Freddie Anderson says the soft look had caught on by the 1920s. [[anderson-sheppard|Anderson & Sheppard]] was founded by Scholte's protégé Per Anderson, with the trouser cutter Sidney Horatio Sheppard.

## The cut

Put This On describes shaped front panels and a chest piece cut on the bias, so the chest stands slightly off the body and spare cloth gathers near the armhole. *The Rake* adds a very soft canvas that supports the cloth without controlling it.

Shoulders and armholes are described differently. Wikipedia says Scholte's shoulders were unpadded and later ones lightly padded; Colin Heywood of Anderson & Sheppard, in *The Rake*, describes a natural line with minimal padding; Put This On calls it soft and sloping, sometimes extended. Wikipedia says the early armhole was roomy and became higher and narrower by the mid-1930s. Heywood describes a small, high armhole with extra fullness in the sleevehead. Wikipedia adds a slightly raised waist forming a V and full pleated trousers.

Wikipedia, citing Hill's *American Menswear*, separates two versions. The English coat has a less tapered waist, a straight skirt and small puckers at the sleevehead; the American one is more tapered, with a flared skirt and a smooth roll at the armscye. Heywood says [[neapolitan-tailoring|Neapolitan]] tailors use less drape.

## Spread

Wikipedia says the Prince's wearing it helped take it to America. Put This On follows it through Hollywood and jazz musicians to the [[zoot-suit]]. The [[bold-look|Bold Look]] of the 1950s kept it going, and the 1974 film *The Great Gatsby* revived it. Anderson & Sheppard, Steed and Steven Hitchcock cut it today.
