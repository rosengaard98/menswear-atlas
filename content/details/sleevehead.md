A well-made jacket has a sleeve that is bigger than the hole it goes into. Simon Crompton of Permanent Style explains why: a small [[armhole]] separates sleeve from body, so the arm can move without dragging the jacket with it. The price is surplus cloth at the top of the sleeve, and it has to go somewhere. Crompton mentions that the edge is wetted before pressing and that the cloth shrinks slightly under the iron.

## Three finishes

Crompton names three ways to deal with the fullness. In the smooth finish the top is clean and the fullness is pushed down to the bottom of the armhole. In the roped finish a thick wad of padding sits at the top. In the [[neapolitan-tailoring|Neapolitan]] finish the fullness falls in many small ripples.

*The Rake* describes British [[drape-cut|drape]] as a small, high armhole with extra fullness in the sleeve head; Colin Heywood of [[anderson-sheppard|Anderson & Sheppard]] tells it the high armhole keeps the coat in place and lets the arm move freely. For Naples, *The Rake* describes a much larger sleeve head carefully fed and hand-stitched into a snug armhole, work it says machines cannot do. The puckering that results is called grinze; some see a flaw, connoisseurs see craftsmanship. Wikipedia says the sleeve on informal Neapolitan jackets is cut about 10 cm larger than the armhole, the shirring is the repecchia, and the flare is called manica a mappina. No source here gives a figure for English or American coats. See [[spalla-camicia|spalla camicia]] for how the gathers relate to that term.

## Con rollino

On formal Neapolitan jackets Wikipedia describes the rollino as a little roll of padding that raises the sleeve head so it drapes more cleanly. *The Rake* says con rollino hides the grinze either with padding or by stretching and heating the cloth. The Styleforum primer says wadding may go in. Whether rollino is the same thing as English roping is unsettled. Posters on The London Lounge forum disagree: one says rope, rollino and the French cigarette name one finish, another that rope comes from easing a slightly larger sleeve head and filling the bulge with soft wadding. None of the sources settles it.

## English or American puckers

The sources also disagree about which side of the Atlantic puckered. Wikipedia, citing D. D. Hill's *American Menswear* (2011), says the sleeve head was the main difference between American and British drape: the American coat had a smooth roll of sleeve cloth at the armscye, the English one tiny puckers from stitch tucks. Gentleman's Gazette captions it the other way round, giving the American version "wrinkles in the sleevehead", without a source or explanation. See also [[natural-shoulder|natural shoulder]].
