Madras was a trade cloth long before it was a summer shirt. Madras Musings quotes the historian Sriram saying that the East India Company's Madras, now [[chennai|Chennai]], was founded for cloth. Francis Day chose Madraspatnam partly because its fabric was about 15 per cent cheaper than at Armagaon, whose cloth Heddels calls too poor to export. Fort St George followed in 1639.

## The Indian cloth

Madras is a light cotton, handwoven in [[india|India]] and usually checked or plaid. Wikipedia says the genuine cloth looks the same on both sides and has slubs from short-staple yarn. The [[east-india-company|Company]] is said to have offered weavers 30 years free of duty, and nearly 400 weaver families settled within a year. Checks may be older than the British: the textile scholar Jasleen Dhamija, cited by Madras Musings, points to 16th-century Portuguese records of checked scarves in the region.

The export cloth was the Real Madras Handkerchief, or RMHK, also called George cloth, Guinea cloth and Injiri. Madras Musings describes bolts of 8 metres, 36 inches wide, cut into three square kerchiefs. The warp was dyed with indigo and the yellow came from turmeric; where the two met, green ran into the weft when the cloth got wet. The V&A says madras checks were a large part of British trade with West Africa and travelled with the slave trade to the West Indies. Its example is a handkerchief of about 1855, made in Mangalore by the German Mission. The Kalabari of Nigeria have traded in it for about 400 years.

Heddels says plaids dominated by 1822, a fashion it links to [[george-iv|George IV]]'s visit to Scotland. Outlook Traveller says the weavers copied the [[tartan|tartans]] of Scottish regiments in Madras.

## Bleeding madras

Heddels says Elihu Yale, governor of Madras, gave madras to the Collegiate School in 1718; Wikipedia gives the 1718 gift without naming him. Heddels gives firmer American markers: an 1897 Sears catalogue, a 1919 *New York Times* report of a shortage, Bahamas holiday wear for young men in the 1930s and 1940s, and a feature in *Gentry* in 1953.

In 1958 [[brooks-brothers|Brooks Brothers]] bought 10,000 yards of vegetable-dyed Indian madras from an importer. The washing warning never reached customers, and the colours ran. Then *Seventeen* ran a feature called "Bleeding Madras", and orders flooded in. Accounts name the importer's supplier differently. In the 1960s David Ogilvy sold [[hathaway|Hathaway]] madras shirts with the line "guaranteed to bleed".

Madras Musings puts the peak in the 1960s. Machine-made imitations from Japan, Switzerland, England and France followed, and Nigerian import bans in the 1970s collapsed the RMHK trade. Wikipedia counts it among the cloths of [[preppy]] dress.
