The name means "shirt shoulder", and it describes a join. On a [[neapolitan-tailoring|Neapolitan]] jacket the sleeve is set so that it looks as if it runs under the shoulder, the way a shirt sleeve does. Simon Crompton of Permanent Style insists that this is all the term means. By itself it does not promise a small shoulder, a [[natural-shoulder|natural shoulder]] or extra gathering at the top of the sleeve.

## How it is made

There is no shoulder pad. The [[sleevehead|sleeve head]] is cut larger than the [[armhole]] and the excess is eased in, which leaves small puckers. The Vou calls these repecchie and says the upper part of the sleeve head, the tromba, is wider than the lower part, the scalfo. Wikipedia puts the extra at about 10 cm, gathered into the repecchia to give the flare called manica a mappina. The Rake describes a much larger sleeve head hand-stitched into a snug armhole, and calls the gathering grinze.

Parisian Gentleman says the seam allowance is turned toward the shoulder rather than the sleeve, as in shirtmaking, and the Styleforum primer says the allowances are pressed toward the body. The Vou presents the puckers as deliberate; The Rake says the larger sleeve inevitably shirrs and that enthusiasts value it.

## Camicia and mappina

The sources do not agree on where the term stops. The Vou and The Rake treat the shirring as part of spalla camicia, while Wikipedia says the sleeve can be finished with the repecchia. In a reply to readers, Crompton separates the camicia, the join, from the mappina, the amount of shirring. A jacket can have either, both or neither, and he notes that tailors themselves usually assume shirring when they say camicia.

A variant called con rollino puts a roll of wadding or shaped cloth at the sleeve head. The Rake says the wadding reduces the shirring, and Styleforum says the sleeve sits raised. Wikipedia ties it to formal jackets.

## Who made it

The usual story belongs to the soft Neapolitan jacket as a whole, not to the shoulder alone. Parisian Gentleman says [[vincenzo-attolini|Vincenzo Attolini]], head cutter at [[rubinacci|Rubinacci]]'s London House in Naples, stripped out pads and linings. Wikipedia places Attolini and Caggiula turning from the British model in the 1920s. The Rake gives the 1930s, with Gennaro Rubinacci asking Attolini for a lighter jacket, and tells it as received history. None of these pages dates the shoulder itself. The Vou names Mariano Rubinacci and [[cesare-attolini|Cesare Attolini]] as masters of it, and [[kiton|Kiton]] among the ready-to-wear makers.
