A smaller armhole gives the arm more freedom, although a bigger one is often assumed to. Simon Crompton of Permanent Style, Hugo Jacomet of Parisian Gentleman and Colin Heywood of [[anderson-sheppard|Anderson & Sheppard]] all say so. With a high, narrow armhole the arm moves while the chest, shoulders and padding of the jacket stay put. A low one leaves spare cloth under the arm, and the whole body of the coat rides up when the arms are lifted or reached forward.

## How high

Height is judged at the bottom of the armhole, under the arm. Crompton says it should sit as high as the wearer's body allows, so what counts as too high varies, and that past that point it pushes the cloth into folds. No source opened for this entry gives a standard measurement. Jacomet adds that a high armhole makes a man stand straighter and his arms look longer, though bending to lace shoes is harder. His test is to raise the arms as if hanging a picture: a good jacket does not move. He names [[cifonelli|Cifonelli]] and [[camps-de-luca|Camps de Luca]] in Paris as known for high armholes, and says armhole height varies widely between makers at every price, so it is worth checking in ready-to-wear too. Crompton says few ready-to-wear makers offer a high armhole because it needs more handwork.

## Big sleeve, small hole

The craft is in setting a large sleeve into the small opening. Crompton calls the small armhole one of the most important functional parts of a bespoke suit, because it separates the sleeve from the body. The extra cloth is eased into the [[sleevehead]]: smooth, roped or rippled in the Neapolitan manner (see [[spalla-camicia|spalla camicia]] and [[neapolitan-tailoring|Naples]]). The Rake says the fullness of the [[drape-cut|drape]] goes into the sleevehead, and Heywood that the high armhole lets the body "fit like a glove". Crompton's 2018 breakdown of an Anderson & Sheppard jacket calls the armhole small and the sleeve wide at the top, narrowing to about 11 1/2 inches.

## Roomy, then raised

The drape did not start this way. Wikipedia says [[frederick-scholte|Frederick Scholte]] took roomy armholes from Household Cavalry uniform, a story more than a record, and that by the mid-1930s drape armholes had been raised and narrowed. No source says who made the change.
