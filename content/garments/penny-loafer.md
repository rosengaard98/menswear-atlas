The American campus shoe has Norwegian roots. In Aurland, in Sogn, the shoemaker Nils Gregoriusson Tveranger (1874 to 1953) is credited with the Aurland shoe, which combined the [[moccasin]] ideas he had met in America with the local slip-on called the tese. Analog Shift says he went to Boston in 1891 and came home in 1894. The dates for the shoe disagree: Wikipedia and Gentleman's Gazette say he designed it around 1908 and won a diploma at a Bergen exhibition in 1910, Port says he patented it as early as 1920, and Scan Magazine, writing about the Aurland factory, gives 1930. None of these is confirmed. Port says English salmon fishers noticed the shoes, and Scan that repairs for British visitors on fishing trips set local makers going. Scan says factories around Aurland made about 100,000 pairs in 1950.

## The Weejun

Analog Shift tells how an *[[esquire|Esquire]]* staffer saw the shoe in Palm Beach and the editor Arnold Gingrich went looking for an American maker, with the New York store [[rogers-peet|Rogers Peet]] as the first retail partner and [[g-h-bass|G.H. Bass]] of Wilton, Maine, as the maker. Port says Esquire and Rogers Peet sought an American manufacturer in the mid-1930s. Bass advertised its Weejun in the *New York Herald Tribune* on 27 May 1936 at $6.50; Wikipedia gives 1934 in its text and 1936 in its table. The name is said to come from "Norwegian".

By the 1950s the Weejun was worn by American prep-school and college students, part of the [[ivy-league-look]] in Analog Shift's account, and the name penny loafer came from the campuses. Port says nobody knows exactly why the fashion started.

## The penny

The Weejun has a saddle, a leather strap across the instep, with a slot in it; Wikipedia and Gentleman's Gazette describe a diamond-shaped slit by the 1950s. Why a penny went there is a matter of stories. The Royal Mint tells the pay-phone version; Analog Shift points out that Bell pay phones took dimes, and Gentleman's Gazette that a call cost more than a cent. The Gazette's review says early students kept dimes there and pennies came later, and its history piece guesses the copper simply looked good. Scan says explanations range from a fashion detail to a symbol of wartime resistance.

## How it is made

Bass says the Weejun upper is cut in four parts to form a tubular moccasin and stitched by hand. Its beefroll, sewn with two needles, gathers the vamp edge into a crimped roll. The leather sole is stitched through the upper and its edge trimmed and stained. Gentleman's Gazette notes that today's Larson has beefrolls on the saddle and the Logan a flat strap. Port names the [[tassel-loafer]], from 1950s London, as a related style.
