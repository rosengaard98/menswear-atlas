Loden is often called felt, and Bernhard Roetzel, writing in Permanent Style, says that is wrong. It is woven first and then fulled: soaked in hot water and soap and pounded until the surface thickens, the weave disappears and the cloth keeps out wind and water. Wikipedia adds that it is brushed with fuller's teasel and clipped, several times over, and classes it as a kind of melton.

## The cloth

The name comes from the Old High German *lodo*, rough cloth. Permanent Style says Alpine fulling mills go back to about the 15th century: peasants spun their own sheep's wool, travelling weavers wove it and a local mill fulled it. Britannica puts peasant handweaving in the 16th century. The Austria-Forum lexicon places the small mills mainly in Styria and [[tyrol|Tyrol]]. The first cloth was coarse mountain-sheep wool; merino, cashmere or angora came in during the first half of the 20th century for lighter versions.

Grey, green and brown were the first colours, and Schladminger grey is the best-known grey. Green is the colour the world links with loden, largely through the Hubertus coat, and was long worn by huntsmen and foresters in Germany and Austria. The cloth is quiet, Roetzel notes, which suits hunting. In the mid-19th century production grew and Austrian royalty made loden country wear, as Victoria did tweed in Roetzel's comparison; under Emperor Franz Josef it settled among noble families. [[lodenfrey|Lodenfrey]] of Munich says it wove a truly water-repellent loden, Strichloden, in 1872. That is the firm's own claim.

## The coat

Britannica calls the coat Tyrolean: thick, soft, waterproof, most often bluish green, sometimes with a stand-up collar and braid or silver buttons. Roetzel says there is no single original Hubertus design, but the usual one has a deep [[inverted-pleat|inverted pleat]] at the back, first meant for shooting, vents under the arms, leather football buttons and pockets with inner openings to reach the clothes beneath. The shoulder is often doubled against rain, and the coat is traditionally unlined, because lining wears out faster than the cloth. He likens it to a slightly unusual [[covert-coat|covert coat]]. The [[wetterfleck|Wetterfleck]], a loden cape, shares its collar. Wikipedia counts loden among [[tracht|Tracht]] and compares the Belgian [[duffel-coat|duffel]] cloth.

The [[duke-of-edinburgh|Duke of Edinburgh]] wore one often. In Britain Permanent Style names Cordings, "a very English retailer", as selling Hubertus coats, and Chrysalis and Private White VC as working in loden. Roetzel finds the coat popular with classically dressed men in Italy, Spain, France and Switzerland.
