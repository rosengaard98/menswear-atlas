The sack coat is half a century older than the Brooks Brothers suit that made it famous. The Museum at FIT dates the American sack suit, which the British call the [[lounge-suit|lounge suit]], to the 1840s and 1850s, when it was leisure wear for the country and private life and had no place in formal town dress. A history on the Lily Absinthe blog, citing museum examples and an 1870 *West End Gazette*, says the coat began in France as the "sacque" in the 1840s.

Lily Absinthe says the name refers to the cut: the back is made of two panels where a [[frock-coat|frock coat]] had four. Wikipedia traces the jacket to the [[paletot]], a loose coat with no waist seam, and describes the sack as the North American cut of the lounge suit: loose, unfitted except at the shoulders, with no darts.

## From leisure to business

FIT says ready-to-wear spread the sack suit across classes from the 1860s, and by the 1890s it was a serious rival to the frock coat and the [[morning-coat|morning coat]]. Daniel Delis Hill, quoted by FIT, says nearly every American man owned at least one matching sack suit by 1900. American fronts were squared, British ones rounded. Lily Absinthe says early versions were often worn as three-piece suits with a waistcoat, with three or four buttonholes and often buttoned only at the top.

## Brooks Brothers

[[brooks-brothers|Brooks Brothers]] did not invent the sack coat. It made a plain version and called it the No. 1 Sack Suit. Die, Workwear! and Bonne Gueule both date it to 1901. The firm's own anniversary page gives no year, saying only that the suit defined American style for the first half of the 20th century. Wikipedia's Brooks Brothers article gives an earlier date, saying the firm introduced the sack suit in 1895. Die, Workwear! calls it the firm's first mass-produced menswear item, worn by store clerks before it reached boardrooms.

## The Ivy sack

The version most people picture belongs to the [[ivy-league-look]]. Wikipedia describes a single-breasted [[three-roll-two]] front with the top button rolled under the lapel, no front darts, a single [[hooked-vent|hooked vent]], a boxy, lightly structured body and a [[natural-shoulder|natural shoulder]]. The trousers sat lower, were belted, plain-fronted and uncuffed. Bonne Gueule repeats the story that Princeton students of the 1920s rolled the lapel to hide the third button, and itself calls it unverified. Ivy style grew on campuses from about the 1920s to the 1940s and peaked in the late 1950s, with Brooks and [[j-press|J. Press]] as major sellers; one figure for 1957-58 puts about 70 per cent of suits sold in the Ivy style.
