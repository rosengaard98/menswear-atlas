In the 1890s [[john-e-brooks|John E. Brooks]], grandson of the founder of [[brooks-brothers]], watched English [[polo|polo]] players whose collar points were buttoned to their shirts so they would not flap in the wind. He sent a sample collar back to the New York store with instructions to copy it. Brooks Brothers dates its button-down to 1896; other accounts put the trip and the first sales around 1900.

The first versions were pullover sport shirts, sold as "polo shirts", a name that a different shirt later took over (see [[polo-shirt]]). The collar was then put on white cotton cheviot, which the trade called oxford, and the shirt became a coat-style shirt that buttons all the way down, first with five buttons and then six.

By 1915 it was a staple at almost every East Coast college. After the Second World War it was the most popular shirt style in America and a pillar of the [[ivy-league-look]] of the late 1940s and 1950s.

## What makes it

The shirt is a sum of small decisions. The [[button-down-collar]] has long points and no interlining, so it [[collar-roll|rolls]] rather than lying flat. The cloth is [[oxford-cloth]], and Brooks had a heavy version woven for its own shirts. At the back a [[box-pleat]] hangs from the [[yoke]]. A fold-over [[front-placket]] keeps the buttons in line, and the sleeves are pleated into plain [[barrel-cuff|barrel cuffs]].

Some familiar details came later. Early shirts had no [[chest-pocket]]; one appears in the 1960s. The [[locker-loop]] arrived on campus shirts in the same decade, while the [[back-collar-button]] went the other way and was dropped by Brooks in the late 1960s.

## How it changed

Enthusiasts call the Brooks shirts made before the 1990s the golden era. Around then Brooks added a seventh front button and an interlining to the collar, which made it stiffer and changed the roll. Non-iron oxford followed, which Put This On called the death knell for the shirt's lived-in character.
