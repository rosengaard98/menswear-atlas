The lounge suit was on sale decades before anyone credited a prince with it. Cathcart London's journal finds Samuels Brothers advertising "seaside and country lounging suits" in 1853, and Browne & Payne of Dublin calling the lounge suit the leading style in 1859.

## Origins

The accounts differ, and none names an inventor. Wikipedia calls it 19th-century British sport and country clothing and traces the jacket to the [[paletot]], shaped by darting the front panels. A V&A caption, reproduced on Historical Menswear, says it grew from the lounging jacket of the 1860s, fitted to the waist with a long dart instead of a waist seam, and was worn with matching waistcoat and trousers by the 1870s. Gentleman's Gazette places it in Scotland in the 1850s and 1860s, and the Lily Absinthe blog in France in the 1840s; neither gives a reference.

The matching cloth set it apart. [[frock-coat|Frock coats]] and [[morning-coat|morning coats]] were usually worn with contrasting trousers, and the matching suit was colloquially called "dittos". Gazette says the Victorian frock coat split in two: the morning coat kept the tails and the lounge suit dropped them. In America the same garment is the [[sack-suit|sack suit]], which FIT equates with the British lounge suit.

## The cut

Cathcart says the *[[tailor-and-cutter|Tailor & Cutter]]* of 1880 favoured a cutaway front with four or five buttons and four flapped pockets, usually in Scottish check tweed. Through the 1880s the fastening dropped and edge stitching replaced bound edges. Late-1890s coats were longer, with little waist suppression and rear vents again. A 1902 coat was single-breasted with small lapels, a high opening and no vents; a 1909 survey found most with a single vent and two hip pockets.

## Taking over

Cathcart says the Prince of Wales, later [[edward-vii|Edward VII]], was reported in lounge suits in the 1880s, and argues he set no trend in 1907-08, since ordinary men had worn them for over fifty years. The date of the takeover varies. FIT calls the sack suit a serious rival to frock and morning coats by the 1890s. Cathcart reports matching suits falling from upper-class favour in the late 1890s, yet flannel lounge suits outnumbering frock and morning coats in the West End by 1905. Wikipedia and the V&A caption say it replaced the frock coat in the early 20th century, and Gazette says that in the [[edwardian]] years the older coats were left mostly to older men.

Two museum records need care. The Met's record titled "Lounge suit", from the late 1840s, is discussed on its page alongside lounging robes in the banyan tradition. The V&A dates Max Beerbohm's lounge suit to 1918-20, though Historical Menswear captions a Beerbohm suit 1882.
