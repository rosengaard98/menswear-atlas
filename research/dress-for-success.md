# Dress for Success (context)

## Claims
- John T. Molloy's *Dress for Success* was first published in the US in 1975 by Peter H. Wyden, New York. — [Dress for Success (Encyclopedia of Clothing and Fashion), Joanne Entwistle](https://www.encyclopedia.com/fashion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/dress-success); [Wikipedia: Dress for Success (book)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_for_Success_(book))
- It was a bestseller. No source opened gives sales figures. — [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/magazine/2018/09/19/forty-years-later-advice-dress-for-success-old-hat/qPAJ8hKAXvDE55b7XBcqLO/story.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_for_Success_(book))
- Molloy called himself a "wardrobe engineer" and said the men's book rested on 15 years of research (his own claim, as reported by Tina Sutton). — [Boston Globe, 2018](https://www.bostonglobe.com/magazine/2018/09/19/forty-years-later-advice-dress-for-success-old-hat/qPAJ8hKAXvDE55b7XBcqLO/story.html)
- Method as described: showing drawings or photographs of outfits to people and recording what they thought of the wearer (competence, fit with other employees); test reactions to garments such as a beige versus a black raincoat; surveys of what men wore. Wikipedia says "based on scientific research"; Entwistle calls it a (pseudo) "science of clothing" built on quantitative "testing"; Alan Pell Crawford says Molloy likened it to later focus groups. Lurie's 1978 review notes Molloy's own claim of "scientific research and opinion-polling" and does not assess his sample. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_for_Success_(book)); [Christian Science Monitor, 9 Feb 1988](https://www.csmonitor.com/1988/0209/fmoll.html); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/fashion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/dress-success); [The American Conservative, 27 Sep 2025](https://www.theamericanconservative.com/from-ties-to-tees/); [NYRB, 7 Dec 1978](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1978/12/07/from-rags-to-rags/)
- Men's advice (per Encyclopedia.com): black or gray suits, not-too-daring ties, polished shoes. Per Crawford: a business suit is "always a good bet" if one's superiors wear it; conformity to "conservative, class-conscious" dress mattered; critics called it encouraging the "executive herd-instinct", which Molloy admitted. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/fashion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/dress-success); [The American Conservative](https://www.theamericanconservative.com/from-ties-to-tees/)
- Exceptions: Madison Avenue creative types could wear a sport coat with pocket square and loafers instead of wingtips. A polka-dot tie is wrong for a non-Southerner selling in the South; letting wife or girlfriend choose clothes was ranked the worst men's mistake. — [The American Conservative](https://www.theamericanconservative.com/from-ties-to-tees/); [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/magazine/2018/09/19/forty-years-later-advice-dress-for-success-old-hat/qPAJ8hKAXvDE55b7XBcqLO/story.html)
- Later Molloy findings (from his 1988 interview, about the revised *New Dress for Success*, Warner Books): bow ties read as "outsider"; red ties as "honest" if closer to maroon than bright red; mustard looks chintzy; a beige suit with a gray tinge works best nationally (greenish in the South); coordination of the whole look matters; contrasting-collar shirts rose from 1 percent to over 60 percent of men (his survey claim). — [Christian Science Monitor, 1988](https://www.csmonitor.com/1988/0209/fmoll.html)
- The same interview says presidential candidates' ties came out the wrong red because consultants misread his first book (Molloy's claim). — [CSM](https://www.csmonitor.com/1988/0209/fmoll.html)
- After 1975 US companies hired him mainly to package salespeople; Japanese-run firms wanted uniform design. A 1980s factory strike made him regret that his advice had dressed executives who cut pay. — [CSM](https://www.csmonitor.com/1988/0209/fmoll.html)
- *The Woman's Dress for Success Book* followed in 1977 (Warner Books paperback edition reviewed in 1978, 187 pp., $3.95). Advice: skirted suit in medium gray or navy wool, modest blouse, no sweaters, pantsuits or cleavage; navy suit, white man-tailored shirt, scarf tied as an ascot. — [NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1978/12/07/from-rags-to-rags/); [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/magazine/2018/09/19/forty-years-later-advice-dress-for-success-old-hat/qPAJ8hKAXvDE55b7XBcqLO/story.html)
- Entwistle dates the women's book inconsistently (1979 and 1980 in her entry); the 1977 date is given by Wikipedia, the Boston Globe, NYRB and Power dressing. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/fashion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/dress-success)
- Influence: the two books popularised "power dressing" and helped start the 1980s power suit with shoulder pads in gray, blue or navy; they fed an image-consultancy industry and charity dress-for-success shops; Filene's in Boston set up a Corporate Image department partly because of the women's book (1979 onward). The backlash came with dress-down Fridays in the 1990s; Crawford ties the book's afterlife to casual dress, Zoom and Silicon Valley style. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/fashion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/dress-success); [Wikipedia: Power dressing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_dressing); [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/magazine/2018/09/19/forty-years-later-advice-dress-for-success-old-hat/qPAJ8hKAXvDE55b7XBcqLO/story.html); [The American Conservative](https://www.theamericanconservative.com/from-ties-to-tees/)
- Lurie (1978) did her own informal polling and says it backed Molloy; a bank executive told her the advantage vanishes when everyone dresses alike. — [NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1978/12/07/from-rags-to-rags/)

## Disagreements and myths
- Women's book date: 1977 (Wikipedia, Power dressing, Boston Globe) vs 1979/1980 (Entwistle, internally inconsistent).
- Method: Molloy says scientific research and polling (15 years); Entwistle calls it pseudo-science; Lurie found her own polling agreed. No source opened describes sample sizes.
- "Marked the end of the peacock revolution" is a framing, not a documented cause; the opened sources do not discuss the peacock revolution at all. Chronology also differs: the existing entry ends it in 1975 yet the book is the endpoint.
- The Boston Globe says the men's book was a 1975 bestseller; no source gives copies sold.
- Not verified: the specific "navy and gray, white shirt, regimental tie" rules, polyester warnings, and Molloy's life dates. I did not open a page stating them.
