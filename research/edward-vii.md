# Edward VII (person)

## Claims
- Henry Poole & Co became his chief tailor after his first visit in 1860 (the page quotes the Duke of Windsor on this). The firm lists his innovations as the Prince of Wales check, the Homburg, the Norfolk jacket and black tie with evening tails. This is the maker's own claim. — [HM King Edward VII](https://henrypoole.com/individual/hm-king-edward-vii)
- Henry Poole says it cut a prototype blue silk smoking jacket for him in 1865 for private dinners at Sandringham, as an informal alternative to the white tie tailcoat. Maker's claim. — [HM King Edward VII](https://henrypoole.com/individual/hm-king-edward-vii)
- Favourbrook says that in 1865 he asked Poole for a shorter evening coat for informal Sandringham dinners, and that Poole's records call it a "celestial-blue smoking jacket with matching trousers". The tale that James Potter took the idea to the Tuxedo Park Club in New York is given only as "reportedly". — [Decoding the dinner jacket](https://favourbrook.com/blogs/journal/decoding-the-dinner-jacket)
- Wikipedia says Poole is "widely believed" to have designed the first modern dinner jacket in the 1860s to the Prince's specifications (citing The Gentleman's Journal). It gives 1860 for the smoking jacket and 1886 for Potter's visit to Sandringham, where the Prince suggested he have one made by Poole; the US name tuxedo follows from Potter wearing it at the Tuxedo Club. Note the date disagreement: 1860 (Wikipedia) against 1865 (Poole, Favourbrook). — [Henry Poole & Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Poole_%26_Co)
- Homburg: the Met says that as Prince of Wales he popularised the hat in England after bringing it back from regular trips to the spa at Homburg, Germany. Wikipedia says the same in more hedged terms (late 19th century, "reportedly" brought one back) and presents it as a less formal alternative to the top hat. — [Homburg, Willoughby Hatter, ca. 1955](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/157802); [Homburg hat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg_hat)
- Turn-ups: Parisian Gentleman says he "purportedly" pioneered the turn-up in the 1890s, with the cuff tailored in rather than rolled by hand, and a back shorter than the front. Gentleman's Gazette says the Prince of Wales introduced the permanent turn-up in 1890, for fashion not weather, with no source beyond Wikipedia. The practical turned-up hem for mud is said to be older. — [A Second Look at Trouser Turnups](https://parisiangentleman.com/blog/a-second-look-at-trouser-turnups-cuffs); [Should you wear cuffed pants?](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/should-you-wear-cuffed-pants/)
- Parisian Gentleman notes photographs are inconclusive: one c.1900 image seems to show cuffs, a postcard shows none, and full-length pictures are scarce. — [A Second Look at Trouser Turnups](https://parisiangentleman.com/blog/a-second-look-at-trouser-turnups-cuffs)
- Side creases: a 1922 Chicago Tribune report quoted by Put This On says "the late King Edward" occasionally wore side-creased trousers (not named as Edward VII by the page; the date makes it likely). Put This On gets it from Kiyoshi Martinez's 2013 post and the book *Savile Row: An Illustrated History*. I found no source for him inventing the front crease. — [Side-creased trousers and King George V](https://www.putthison.com/?p=14912)
- Glen Urquhart: Wikipedia says the check was first used in the 1840s by the Countess of Seafield on the Glenurquhart estate to dress gamekeepers, and was "first made famous" by Edward VII as Prince of Wales, citing the Parisian Gentleman blog. The name "Glen plaid" is not found before 1926. Edward VIII popularised it later. — [Glen plaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen_plaid)
- Waistcoat bottom button: Mental Floss states as fact that the Prince grew too stout to fasten it and kept it open because he liked the look, with no source. Wikipedia's Waistcoat article calls the Edward explanation a myth, citing a 2012 GQ piece by Robert Johnston. Henry Poole mentions only that he left the lowest button undone, linked to his corpulence and the nickname "Tum-Tum". — [What's the origin of the sometimes-always-never rule](https://www.mentalfloss.com/culture/fashion-beauty/whats-origin-sometimes-always-never-rule-buttoning-suit); [Waistcoat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waistcoat); [HM King Edward VII](https://henrypoole.com/individual/hm-king-edward-vii)
- Royal Collection Trust: his wife Alexandra was described by Vogue as the "legitimate head of fashion" in the dominions. This page has nothing on his own clothes beyond his fancy dress at balls (1871, 1874, 1897). — [The glamorous lives of the Edwardians](https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/the-glamorous-lives-of-the-edwardians)

## Disagreements and myths
- Waistcoat button: stated as fact (Mental Floss), called a myth (Wikipedia, citing GQ). Treat as a story.
- Dinner jacket: 1860 (Wikipedia) against 1865 (Poole, Favourbrook); "widely believed", not documented in what I read. The Potter and Tuxedo Park link is "reportedly". I found no page opened tying it to Cowes; do not use Cowes.
- Turn-ups: 1890 (Gentleman's Gazette) against 1890s "purportedly" (Parisian Gentleman); photographic evidence thin.
- Norfolk jacket at Sandringham: only Poole's list in my sources (a maker's claim); the existing english-country entry already carries Wikipedia's doubt.
- Marienbad: not found in any page I opened. Unverified; leave out.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Turn-up: back of the cuff cut shorter than the front, more cloth sloping to the shoe (Parisian Gentleman).
- Dinner jacket prototype: short, blue silk smoking jacket with matching trousers (Poole's records via Favourbrook).
