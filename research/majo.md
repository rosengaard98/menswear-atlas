# Majo dress / majismo (style)

## Claims
- Majos were working-class Madrileños from poorer districts such as Maravillas (now Malasaña) and Lavapiés; they took pride in Spanish identity, and bullfighting was among their pursuits — [Muy majo, The Making of Madrid](https://themakingofmadrid.com/2018/01/muy-majo-madrids-fiercely-independent-working-class-tribes)
- The V&A's catalogue note says the "majos" were immigrants from other Spanish regions in 18th-century Madrid who brought their regional dress, and that Madrid's elite adopted it as a patriotic Spanish style rejecting French fashion — [Majos suit, V&A](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O358108/majos-suit-suit-unknown)
- Men's majo dress as Velasco Molpeceres lists it: calzón (breeches) and stockings, a jaquetilla or jubón with a faja (sash), natural hair under a redecilla (hairnet), topped by a montera or small hat — [Velasco Molpeceres, Estudios de Historia de España 2020](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/bitstream/123456789/10952/1/afrancesamiento-moda-espanola.pdf)
- The long capa went with the very wide chambergo hat, which let the wearer muffle the face (embozo). The chambergo had little Spanish tradition; the article says it came with General Schömberg's troops and spread via Queen Mariana's guard under Carlos II — [Velasco Molpeceres 2020](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/bitstream/123456789/10952/1/afrancesamiento-moda-espanola.pdf)
- Crown edicts against the long cape are listed for 1716, 1719, 1723, 1729, 1737, 1740 and 1745, all ineffective; after 1766 the cape fell out of fashion, and in the 19th century it became tied to the Spanish gentleman, the theatre and opera, and the more popular classes — [Velasco Molpeceres 2020](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/bitstream/123456789/10952/1/afrancesamiento-moda-espanola.pdf)
- Esquilache's bando banned the broad-brimmed hat and long cape in Madrid (short cape and three-cornered hat required). Reasons given: concealed weapons and faces. The measure applied first to royal functionaries (Wikipedia: 21 January 1766), then to everyone — [Esquilache Riots, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esquilache_Riots); [Cañas, UI1](https://www.ui1.es/blog-ui1/15-de-abril-de-1766-el-motin-de-esquilache-ii-causas-consecuencias-y-conclusiones-del-motin)
- Riots began 23 March 1766 and ended three days later; about forty dead; Esquilache was removed — [Cañas, UI1](https://www.ui1.es/blog-ui1/15-de-abril-de-1766-el-motin-de-esquilache-ii-causas-consecuencias-y-conclusiones-del-motin)
- Afterwards the Count of Aranda reportedly persuaded the guilds that chambergo and long cape belonged to the hangman, and the new styles were accepted — [Esquilache Riots, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esquilache_Riots); Cañas gives the same executioner point, citing López González (1995)
- Majismo proper: aristocrats took up majo/maja dress. Velasco Molpeceres says it mattered most under Carlos IV and was gone by Isabel II, and cites Goya's portraits of María Luisa de Parma and the Duchess of Alba — [Velasco Molpeceres 2020](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/bitstream/123456789/10952/1/afrancesamiento-moda-espanola.pdf)
- A 2021 Complutense thesis (author not shown on the page) studies majismo as upper-class interest in popular dress, 1750-1800, focused on women — [UCM eprints](https://eprints.ucm.es/id/eprint/65166/)
- Rich Madrid children dressed as majos and majas for the San Isidro pilgrimage in the late 18th century — [ESMadrid, The Meadow according to Goya](https://www.esmadrid.com/en/meadow-according-goya)
- Goya's *The Parasol* (c. 1777, oil on linen, 104 x 152 cm, Prado) is a tapestry cartoon: the young man holding the parasol is a majo, hair in a net, colourful silk belt; the woman wears French-style dress. Wikipedia says Goya painted cartoons 1775-1792 for El Pardo — [The Parasol, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Parasol)
- The ESMadrid page notes boys with hair in a net in *Blind Man's Buff* — [ESMadrid](https://www.esmadrid.com/en/meadow-according-goya)
- The V&A holds a majos suit, Spain, 1800-1825, accession 918&A-1901, maker unknown, catalogued as menswear/fancy dress: pink silk satin waist-length jacket and breeches trimmed with silver braid and fringe. It was worn as fancy dress in the 19th century — [V&A](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O358108/majos-suit-suit-unknown)
- Bullfighting echo: the traje de luces dates from the 18th century and was based on the showy dress of 18th-century dandies and showmen around the bullfight; Wikipedia says Goya recorded the 18th-century style, and a "Goyaesque" bullfight survives with bicorne, capote and silk taleguilla. Pieces: montera, corbatín, chaquetilla, taleguilla, capote de paseo, coleta — [Traje de luces, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traje_de_luces)
- Phoenix Art Museum's blog says the suit was influenced by the dress of Spain's lower classes, and that lower-ranking bullfighters wore silver and elite ones gold — [The History of el Traje de Luces, PhxArt](https://phxart.org/blog/the-history-of-el-traje-de-luces/)

## Disagreements and myths
- Date of the bando: Cañas says 20 March 1766, noting others give the 10th; Wikipedia and Velasco Molpeceres say 10 March. Cañas also mentions a 1745 order already forbidding it.
- Outcome: The Making of Madrid says Charles withdrew the decree; Wikipedia says the king conceded to demands and Esquilache was removed, but the dress reform was then enforced by Aranda via the hangman argument; Cañas (citing López González 1995) says the reform continued. The "decree withdrawn" version is the weaker one.
- Making of Madrid describes majos as "colorful embroidered outfits" and puts hairnets nowhere; its trousers/checked cap details belong to later chulapos, so do not use them for majos.
- Duchess of Alba strolling Madrid as a maja: called unlikely by Making of Madrid. Treat as a story.
- Majismo is mostly studied through women's dress (maja); male-specific detail is thin in what I could open. Needs a Spanish costume source (Museo del Traje, Caro Baroja *Los majos*) for firm garment detail.
- V&A's origin story (regional immigrants) is the museum's catalogue note; Making of Madrid describes majos simply as Madrid working class. Two accounts, not necessarily in conflict.
- The Parasol page on Wikipedia gives c. 1777 for the cartoon; ESMadrid says the San Isidro tapestry commission is 1788. Different works; do not conflate.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- V&A suit (1800-1825, a showy aristocratic/fancy-dress version): jacket pink silk satin, waist length, 7.5 cm standing collar, white silk twill revers, long curved two-piece sleeves laced on with white silk ribbon through eyelets (blue ribbon over the shoulder seams), 6.3 cm silver braid and 8 mm silver fringe on front, pockets, sleeves, revers, collar; front does not fasten, with 6 and 5 blind buttonholes in silver braid. Jacket 50.5 cm collar to hem; chest 96 cm.
- Breeches same silk, back gathered into a 5 cm waistband, narrow fall front, watch pocket right side, silver braid knee bands, 13 buttons and seven buttonholes per knee, 34 filigree silver spheres along the outer leg seams. Length 82.5 cm, waist 75.5 cm.
- Working majo kit (Velasco Molpeceres): calzón, stockings, short jacket (jaquetilla) or jubón, faja at the waist, redecilla over natural hair, montera or small hat; long capa pulled over the face with a wide chambergo.
- Parasol cartoon: majo with hair in a net and a colourful silk belt.
- Traje de luces pieces for the later echo: short rigid chaquetilla with shoulder reinforcement, taleguilla held by tirantes, pink silk over white cotton stockings, corbatín, montera; goldwork over seven layers (PhxArt).
