# Full sleevehead (detail)

Working name: Full sleevehead (sleeve head). Folding a short entry into armhole is possible, but four entries link to it and the three treatments (smooth, roped, rippled) are a clear subject of their own.

## Claims
- A jacket with a small armhole needs a sleeve that is larger than the opening, so the extra cloth has to be worked into the sleeve head somehow. Crompton: the small armhole separates sleeve from body, so the sleeve moves "without dragging the body with it". — [The work in a bespoke jacket: Part 3 (sleeves)](https://www.permanentstyle.com/?p=96473)
- Crompton (Permanent Style, September 2024) names three finishes: smooth and clean with all the fullness at the bottom; roped, with a thick wad of padding at the top, giving a rounded, structured shoulder; and Neapolitan, where the fullness falls in many small ripples. The page has a video of tailor Jennie Adamson making a sleeve head but gives no steps in text, no amount of ease and no wadding sizes. — [Part 3 (sleeves)](https://www.permanentstyle.com/?p=96473)
- The same page says the sleeve edge is wetted before pressing and that the cloth shrinks slightly when ironed (Crompton's reply to a reader about the video). — [Part 3 (sleeves)](https://www.permanentstyle.com/?p=96473)
- Colin Heywood of Anderson & Sheppard (The Rake, 2017): British drape uses a small, high armhole and puts the extra fullness in the sleeve head. — [The Timeless Elegance of British Drape](https://therake.com/stories/british-drape)
- Wikipedia's drape suit page, citing D. D. Hill, *American Menswear* (2011), pp. 174-175: the main difference between the American and British drape was the sleeve head at the shoulder line. The American coat had a smooth roll of sleeve cloth stitched into the armscye; the English one had tiny puckers from stitch tucks. — [Drape suit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit)
- Neapolitan: The Rake (Christian Barker, 2024) says a much larger sleeve head is "carefully fed and handstitched" into a high, snug armhole, and that machines cannot do it. The resulting gathering is called grinze, a puckered, rippling texture; some see it as a flaw, connoisseurs value it as craftsmanship. — [The History and Anatomy of Neapolitan Tailoring](https://therake.com/stories/the-history-and-anatomy-of-neapolitan-tailoring)
- Same article: spalla camicia leaves the grinze visible; con rollino hides them, by padding or by stretching and heating the cloth, giving a raised, roped shoulder. — [Anatomy of Neapolitan Tailoring](https://therake.com/stories/the-history-and-anatomy-of-neapolitan-tailoring)
- Wikipedia's Neapolitan tailoring page: on informal jackets the sleeve is cut about 10 cm larger than the armhole, and the shirring made with that cloth is the repecchia; the resulting flare is called manica a mappina and gives a "rugged" look. For formal occasions the rollino is "a little roll of padding that raises the sleeve head to drape more cleanly". The page does not mention wadding by that word. — [Neapolitan tailoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neapolitan_tailoring)
- Styleforum Journal primer: the shirt-sleeve style gathers cloth beneath the shoulder seam and is comfortable with room to move; the English-inspired style gives the most built-up shoulder; wadding may be placed in the sleeve head of the con rollino style. It does not define rollino and sends readers to another blog. — [Primer: Neapolitan Jacket Shoulder Style](https://journal.styleforum.net/tag/spalla-camicia)
- Forum, not authoritative: London Lounge posters disagree whether rope and rollino are different. One says rope (English), rollino (Italian) and cigarette (French) name one finish, made naturally or with rolled padding; another says rope comes from a slightly larger sleeve head circumference that eases in cloth and forms a bulge, filled with soft wadding. — [rollino vs. rope](https://thelondonlounge.net/forum/viewtopic.php?p=18284)

## Disagreements and myths
- English or American puckers? Wikipedia (citing Hill) gives the English coat puckers and the American a smooth roll. Gentleman's Gazette's drape page captions the American interpretation of English drape as having "wrinkles in the sleevehead", while the London Lounge version has none. The Gazette page opened only mentions this in captions and gives no source or explanation. — [Drape suit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit), [What is Drape?](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/drape-cut/)
- Does rollino mean a padded roll? Wikipedia says a small roll of padding; The Rake says padding or stretch-and-heat shaping of the cloth; forum posters disagree whether it differs from English roping. No source opened settles this.
- Are the ripples part of spalla camicia? Already recorded in content/details/spalla-camicia.md (Crompton separates camicia from mappina); not reopened here.
- Amount of ease: only Wikipedia gives a figure (about 10 cm, informal Neapolitan jackets). No source opened gives ease for English or American coats.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- The sleeve head (sleeve cap) is the top of the sleeve, sewn into the armhole. It is cut to a larger circumference than the opening; the surplus is eased in by hand, shrinking and pressing (Crompton mentions water at the edge, and slight shrinkage on ironing).
- Three looks: smooth (fullness at the bottom of the armhole); roped (padding wad at the top, rounded edge standing proud of the shoulder); rippled (small gathers around the top, Neapolitan).
- Con rollino: a small roll of padding or wadding at the top that raises the sleeve head and hides ripples.
