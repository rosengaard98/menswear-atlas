# Drape cut (detail)

Existing entries: duke-of-windsor (related, Wikipedia: Scholte developed the drape suit for him, 1930s; wide shoulders, roomy armholes, narrow waist from Household Cavalry uniform) and neapolitan-tailoring (influenced-by, The Rake: Rubinacci proposed a softer unlined version of Scholte's and Anderson & Sheppard's drape in the 1930s). I did not re-open those pages' sources except as listed below; the Rubinacci/1930s claim is not re-verified here.

## Claims
- Drape (also called blade cut or London cut) is a British variant of the three-piece suit developed in the 1930s, with its athletic design attributed to Frederick Scholte — [Drape suit (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit)
- Chest: extra cloth through shoulders and chest gives a slight drape or wrinkle; mid-1930s lapel changes added chest fullness — [Drape suit (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit)
- Chest: full, with a distinctive vertical drape that gives the style its name — [The Timeless Elegance of British Drape (The Rake, Ben St George, April 2017)](https://therake.com/stories/british-drape)
- Chest: cut with shaped panels and a chest piece cut on the bias, so the chest stands slightly off the body; surplus cloth gathers near the armhole — [Lean And Clean Vs. Drape And Shape (Put This On, Derek Guy, 19 March 2018)](https://putthison.com/?p=37025)
- Shoulder: Wikipedia says Scholte's shoulders were unpadded, later builds had light padding; The Rake says natural line, cut wide with minimal padding then coming in (Colin Heywood, Anderson & Sheppard); Put This On says soft and sloping, sometimes extended — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit), [The Rake](https://therake.com/stories/british-drape), [Put This On](https://putthison.com/?p=37025)
- Armhole: roomy in Scholte's early style, narrower and raised by the mid-1930s (Wikipedia); small and high with extra fullness given through the sleevehead (The Rake, Heywood: high armhole keeps the coat in place and allows free movement) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit), [The Rake](https://therake.com/stories/british-drape)
- Waist: slightly nipped and raised, forming a V; sleeves fuller, tapered at the wrist; trousers full, pleated, tapering slightly to the ankle — [Drape suit (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit)
- Canvas: a very soft canvas that supports without controlling the cloth — [The Rake](https://therake.com/stories/british-drape)
- Cloth: developed when most cloths weighed 13 oz to 20 oz, which helped them drape (The Rake's statement) — [The Rake](https://therake.com/stories/british-drape)
- English vs American: English version has a less tapered waist and straight skirt, with small puckers at the sleevehead from stitch tucks; American version more tapered, flared skirt, smooth roll of cloth at the armscye (Wikipedia, citing Hill, *American Menswear*, 2011) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit)
- Scholte (born Amsterdam 19 July 1865, died 2 December 1948) ran a Savile Row house; apprenticed to the Household Cavalry's tailors, he adapted wide shoulders, roomy armholes and narrow waists from their uniform; principal tailor to the Prince of Wales 1919–1948; a footnote says sources claiming 1959 are wrong — [Frederick Scholte (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Scholte)
- Put This On's version of the origin: Scholte saw that belting a Guardsman's coat made the chest fuller and the waist nipped, and carried this into his suits — [Put This On](https://putthison.com/?p=37025)
- Anderson & Sheppard: founded by Per (Peter Gustaf) Anderson, a Swedish expatriate and Scholte's protégé, with trouser cutter Sidney Horatio Sheppard; the house calls the English drape the most English Savile Row cut; "115-year-old" in April 2021 implies about 1906 (my inference, not stated as a date) — [Anderson & Sheppard: A light touch (The Rake, Freddie Anderson, April 2021)](https://therake.com/stories/anderson-sheppard-a-light-touch/)
- Spread: worn by Edward VIII, which helped it reach America (Wikipedia); later through Hollywood and jazz musicians, eventually the zoot suit (Put This On). Wikipedia calls the zoot suit an extreme exaggeration. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drape_suit), [Put This On](https://putthison.com/?p=37025)
- Later: Bold Look in the 1950s continued broad-shouldered double-breasted drape (Wikipedia); *The Great Gatsby* (1974) revived it (Wikipedia). Makers now named: Anderson & Sheppard, Steed (Put This On); Steven Hitchcock (The Rake).
- Met holds a Scholte evening suit of the Duke, 1938–65, with duplicate trousers by H. Harris of New York; the page does not mention drape — [Evening suit 1974.185.3a-c (The Met)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/82170)

## Disagreements and myths
- Inventor: Wikipedia and The Rake credit Scholte; a 1917 *Tailor & Cutter* review (quoted via Wikipedia's Scholte page, not opened directly) warned against crediting any one man with a method. Treat "Scholte invented it" as the trade's attribution.
- Date: "1930s" (Wikipedia Drape suit) vs "soft look caught on by the 1920s" and Scholte's softening "at the turn of the 20th century" (The Rake, A&S piece, Freddie Anderson, who writes for the house; a house claim). Wikipedia also lists the late 1920s for the athletic silhouette.
- Shoulder: unpadded (Wikipedia, Scholte) vs minimal padding (The Rake) vs light padding later.
- Armhole: roomy (early, Wikipedia) vs small and high (The Rake, Heywood). Wikipedia reconciles this as a change by the mid-1930s; no source I opened dates it more precisely.
- Anderson's first name: Per (Put This On, Rake A&S piece) vs Peter Gustaf (The Rake 2017/2021 gives both).
- Wikipedia's Drape suit page names no Duke of Windsor and no Anderson & Sheppard; the household-cavalry detail appears on the Scholte page instead.
- No measurements (inches of drape, chest ease) found in any opened source.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Pieces: front panels shaped to leave extra cloth; bias-cut chest piece; soft canvas; sleevehead with extra fullness eased into a small high armhole; waist slightly raised, V shape; peaked or notch lapels (Wikipedia: peak lapels wide and angled upward mid-1930s); single or double breasted; full pleated trousers.
- For the illustrator: the visible mark is a vertical fold running from armhole/chest down toward the waist (Put This On, The Rake). No measurements available; draw proportions, not figures.
