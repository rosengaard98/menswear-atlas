# Polo shirt (garment)

## Claims
- René Lacoste, French tennis champion (seven Grand Slam titles), first wore his short-sleeved piqué cotton shirt at the U.S. Open in 1926 because tennis whites (long sleeves, tie) restricted movement. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/), [Analog Shift](https://www.analogshift.com/blogs/transmissions/polo-shirt-history), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt)
- Lacoste's shirt had a soft unstarched collar that could be turned up against the sun, a buttoned placket, short sleeves and a longer back hem (the "tennis tail") to keep it tucked in. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt)
- The cloth was a loosely knit cotton piqué, called "jersey petit piqué" in Wikipedia's account; Roland-Garros calls it "petit piqué", a fine-mesh cotton knit. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt), [Roland-Garros](https://www.rolandgarros.com/en-us/article/rg-summer-the-polo-shirt-the-other-icon-roland-garros)
- Crocodile: American press nicknamed Lacoste "the Alligator"/"Crocodile" around 1926-27 (reasons uncertain; a bet over a suitcase is one story); he had a crocodile embroidered on a blazer; Wikipedia says the emblem went on the shirt's left breast from 1927. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt)
- 1933: Lacoste and André Gillier (head of a large French knitwear firm) founded La Chemise Lacoste to sell the shirt. Prototype name L.12.12; first ad campaign that year. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/), [Analog Shift](https://www.analogshift.com/blogs/transmissions/polo-shirt-history), [Heddels](https://heddels-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/the-history-of-the-polo-shirt-from-rene-lacoste-through-ralph-lauren)
- Roland-Garros: Lacoste wore it on centre court at Roland-Garros in 1928, released it commercially in 1933; original model L.12.12 had three buttons. — [Roland-Garros](https://www.rolandgarros.com/en-us/article/rg-summer-the-polo-shirt-the-other-icon-roland-garros)
- Lewis Lacey (born Montreal 1887, Argentine-based polo champion) is credited by Wikipedia with creating and selling short-sleeved shirts with a mounted polo player on the left breast for the Hurlingham polo team in the 1923 season. This rests on one cited source (westchestercup.org biography), which I did not open. Wikipedia's polo-shirt page dates his shirt 1920 and links it to a Hurlingham Club emblem near Buenos Aires; the two Wikipedia pages differ on the year. — [Wikipedia: Lewis Lacey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Lacey), [Wikipedia: Polo shirt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt)
- Brooks Brothers: John Brooks saw English polo players with collars buttoned down (to stop flapping) and Brooks introduced the button-down collar in 1896; Heddels says it was marketed as "The Original Button-Down Polo Shirt". This is a woven dress shirt, a different garment. — [Heddels](https://heddels-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/the-history-of-the-polo-shirt-from-rene-lacoste-through-ralph-lauren), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt)
- Smithsonian says the Brooks story is "likely" the source of the name "polo shirt"; it also repeats an account linking the style to British polo players in India. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/)
- US licence: the shirt reached the U.S. in 1952 via Izod (Izod of London), sold as Izod Lacoste, at about $8, which was dear; Vincent De Paul Draddy gave shirts to JFK, Eisenhower and Bing Crosby. Heddels dates the Izod licence 1951. Izod and Lacoste split in 1993. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/), [Heddels](https://heddels-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/the-history-of-the-polo-shirt-from-rene-lacoste-through-ralph-lauren)
- Fred Perry launched a British version (laurel wreath) in the late 1940s per Roland-Garros; Analog Shift says he co-founded his brand in 1952 with Tibby Wegner. — [Roland-Garros](https://www.rolandgarros.com/en-us/article/rg-summer-the-polo-shirt-the-other-icon-roland-garros), [Analog Shift](https://www.analogshift.com/blogs/transmissions/polo-shirt-history)
- Ralph Lauren, 1972: a knitted piqué cotton shirt in 24 colours with a polo-player emblem, tagline "it gets better with age" (Heddels); Analog Shift and Wikipedia agree on 1972 and add that his shirt made "polo shirt" the common name for what was a tennis shirt, "never meant for polo". — [Heddels](https://heddels-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/the-history-of-the-polo-shirt-from-rene-lacoste-through-ralph-lauren), [Analog Shift](https://www.analogshift.com/blogs/transmissions/polo-shirt-history), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo_shirt)
- 1970s-80s: as "Izod" it was central to the preppy look among teenagers; faded by early 1990s. — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-story-behind-the-lacoste-crocodile-shirt-91276898/)
- Immortally Preppy (a blog, modest authority) says polo players adopted the shirt and made it longer in the back for leaning over a horse; it offers that as the way to tell a polo shirt from a tennis shirt. This contradicts the usual tennis-tail origin; treat as unverified. — [Immortally Preppy](https://immortallypreppy.beehiiv.com/p/ren-lacoste-and-the-history-of-the-polo-shirt)

## Disagreements and myths
- First polo-style knit shirt: Lacoste 1926 (widely told, partly his company's story) versus Lewis Lacey 1920 or 1923 (one Wikipedia page each, one unseen cited source). Lacey's shirt is described as a short-sleeved sports shirt with a polo emblem; nothing opened describes its knit or collar. Do not claim he preceded Lacoste as fact.
- Year of the crocodile on the shirt: Wikipedia 1927; Smithsonian says nickname 1926-27 and the logo first on a blazer; the commercial shirt is 1933.
- Izod licence: 1951 (Heddels) versus U.S. arrival 1952 (Smithsonian).
- Name: Smithsonian says the Brooks button-down is the likely origin of "polo shirt"; another account links to British polo players in India. Lacoste's shirt was for tennis, and Lauren's 1972 marketing fixed the name.
- Tennis tail origin: tennis (Smithsonian, Wikipedia) versus polo riding (Immortally Preppy).
- Distinct from OCBD: the OCBD is woven (oxford cloth), has a full front opening and a button-down collar; the polo shirt is a knit pullover with a short placket and a flat or ribbed knit collar. The shared word "polo" comes from players' clothing in both cases.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Original Lacoste (all from Wikipedia, Smithsonian, Analog Shift): white; short sleeves; piqué cotton knit; flat unstarched collar, can be turned up; buttoned placket (three buttons per Roland-Garros and Heddels); longer back hem (tennis tail); emblem on left chest.
- Modern Lacoste L.12.12 Light (Analog Shift): 165 GSM piqué; ribbed collar and sleeves; mother-of-pearl buttons; sewn-on emblem.
- Ralph Lauren stretch mesh (Analog Shift): three-button placket, vented hem, ribbed armbands.
- Other variants (Analog Shift): button-less placket and one-piece collar (Orlebar Brown, terry); two-button placket, full collar band, side gussets (The Armoury).
- Wikipedia: collar usually one-ply ribbed knit; golf versions have a double-layer stitched collar and three/four-button placket and sometimes a left pocket.
- No page opened gives measurements (placket length, collar depth, sleeve length) or says whether Lacoste's 1933 sleeves were ribbed. For the drawing: say plainly the plate shows a classic modern form (flat knit collar, two or three button placket, ribbed sleeve bands, curved hem with small side vents and longer back), unmarked as period-exact.
