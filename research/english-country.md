# English country style (style)

## Claims
- Tweed was first worn as upper-class country clothing, such as shooting jackets; the Edwardian middle classes later took it up with Norfolk jackets and plus-fours for hunting, cycling, golf and early motoring. — [Tweed, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweed)
- The cloth was originally "tweel", Scots for twill. The name "tweed" is said to come from a clerical misreading: Wikipedia dates the story to about 1831 (merchant James Locke, a Hawick letter), Britannica to a London clerk in 1826. Britannica says the name does not come from the River Tweed. — [Tweed, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweed); [Tweed, Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/art/tweed)
- Estate tweeds are district checks made to identify the people of one estate. One source says the first, Glenfeshie, was commissioned around 1835 for ghillies and keepers; another credits Janie Ellice, a Victorian tenant of the Invereshie side of Glen Feshie, who added a scarlet overcheck to shepherd's check. — [Gentleman's Gazette tweed guide](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/tweed-guide-harris-history-styles-patterns/); [Permanent Style, origins of tweed patterns](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2025/12/the-origins-of-tweed-patterns-scottish-estates-and-district-checks.html)
- Victorian visitors renting Scottish estates had staff uniforms made in district checks; they had no link to a local tartan. Johnstons wove many of them. — [Permanent Style](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2025/12/the-origins-of-tweed-patterns-scottish-estates-and-district-checks.html)
- Glen Urquhart check: woven locally by Elizabeth MacDougall, adopted by the Countess of Seafield as the Balmacaan Estate tweed; Edward VII as Prince of Wales adapted it in brown and cream and shrank the scale; Edward VIII added red and blue overchecks, now called Prince of Wales check. — [Permanent Style](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2025/12/the-origins-of-tweed-patterns-scottish-estates-and-district-checks.html)
- Coigach was first woven by Johnstons in 1846; an American gun club made its own version in 1874, giving the check the name gun club. — [Permanent Style](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2025/12/the-origins-of-tweed-patterns-scottish-estates-and-district-checks.html)
- Lovat mixture (12th Lord Lovat) is based on the colours around Loch Morar; Permanent Style says it was partly homage, partly camouflage. — [Permanent Style](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2025/12/the-origins-of-tweed-patterns-scottish-estates-and-district-checks.html)
- Prince Albert is credited with making Scotland a shooting destination and with a Balmoral tweed for deer stalking; he moved into Balmoral in 1853. — [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/tweed-guide-harris-history-styles-patterns/)
- Harris Tweed was introduced to the aristocracy in the 1840s by Lady Catherine Murray, Countess of Dunmore; the Orb certification mark dates from 1909. The Harris Tweed Act 1993 requires pure virgin wool dyed and spun in the islands. — [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/tweed-guide-harris-history-styles-patterns/); [Tweed, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweed)
- Cheviot tweed is hard-wearing and thornproof, linked to gamekeepers and hunting. — [Tweed, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweed)
- Norfolk jacket: loose, belted, single-breasted tweed jacket with box pleats front and back, patch pockets; a shooting coat that lets the wearer raise the elbow to fire. Full and half-belt versions. Fashionable after the 1860s (one theory: invented late 1860s, popularised 1880s). — [Norfolk jacket, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_jacket)
- The belt shrank to a half belt at the back and then vanished, leaving the structure of the modern sports jacket, by the 1930s. This is one retailer's account without cited sources. — [Westwood Hart, origins of the sports jacket](https://westwoodhart.com/blogs/westwood-hart/origins-evolution-sports-jacket-norfolk-modern-history)
- Barbour: founded 1894 in South Shields by John Barbour, a Galloway native, as an oilcloth importer; limited company 1912; made weatherproofs for the military in WWII. Beaufort waxed hunting jacket introduced 1983, adopted by royals, aristocrats and public. Royal warrants: Duke of Edinburgh 1974, Queen Elizabeth II 1982, Charles III 1987. Dates are as given on Wikipedia, which may rest on the company. — [Barbour, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbour_(company))
- Brogues: from rough Irish and Scottish hide shoes; the name is from Gaelic for shoe. Entered English late 16th century; applied to an outdoor walking shoe in the early 20th century. Traditionally country footwear, once unsuitable for business, now accepted in most settings. — [Brogue shoe, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brogue_shoe)
- Tweed's camouflage use and the "no brown in town" rule are commonly cited but treated as background, not verified. — [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/tweed-guide-harris-history-styles-patterns/)

## Connections
- originated-in (style → place/sport): English country style grew from tweed worn for shooting and stalking on estates. — [Tweed, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweed)
- influenced norfolk-jacket → sports jacket: the Norfolk is called the start of the sports coat. — [Westwood Hart](https://westwoodhart.com/blogs/westwood-hart/origins-evolution-sports-jacket-norfolk-modern-history)
- worn-by edward-vii: popularised the Norfolk jacket in his sporting circle at Sandringham; adapted Glen Urquhart check. — [Norfolk jacket, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_jacket); [Permanent Style](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2025/12/the-origins-of-tweed-patterns-scottish-estates-and-district-checks.html)
- introduced-by prince-albert: Balmoral tweed and Scotland as shooting ground. — [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/tweed-guide-harris-history-styles-patterns/)
- made-by barbour (waxed-cotton-jacket); harris-tweed introduced to gentry by Countess of Dunmore.
- belongs-to-era victorian and edwardian (estate tweeds 1830s-1850s, Norfolk 1860s-1880s, middle-class adoption in Edwardian years).

## Disagreements and myths
- Tweed name: 1826 clerk (Britannica) vs about 1831 James Locke (Wikipedia); Gentleman's Gazette calls the story "allegedly" true.
- Norfolk jacket name: Coke of Norfolk (Holkham duck-shooting), the 15th Duke of Norfolk, or the county; Wikipedia says none is well supported. No pictures of the Prince of Wales in one are known there, so the Edward VII link is a claim, not shown.
- First estate tweed: Glenfeshie (c. 1835, ghillies and keepers) vs Janie Ellice's Glen Feshie check; the two may be the same check, but the sources do not say so.
- Lovat/khaki: Permanent Style says Lovat was the forerunner of khaki; its own comments dispute this (khaki from the Corps of Guides, India, c. 1846). Crompton's reply says Lovat was made in 1845 but not used by the army until 1859.
- Camouflage rationale for tweed is asserted without sources in several places.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Norfolk jacket: single-breasted, 3 or 4 buttons, notched lapel, box pleats front and back, patch pockets, full or half belt.
- Estate checks: shepherd's check base (dark/light squares), overchecks in scarlet, red, blue.
- Brogue: perforated upper (decorative today; drainage story is unproven); semi-brogue attributed to John Lobb in the early 1900s on Wikipedia.
- Waxed cotton: oilcloth lineage; Beaufort is a hunting jacket.
