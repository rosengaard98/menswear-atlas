# Sack suit (garment)

## Claims
- The sack coat is a 19th-century garment, well before any Brooks Brothers suit. FIT dates the American sack suit (British: lounge suit) to the 1840s-50s as leisurewear for country or private life, not formal in cities. — [Sargent, Mr Stokes, 1897](https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1897-sargent-mr-stokes/)
- From the 1860s ready-to-wear spread the sack suit across classes. By the 1890s it rivalled the frock and morning coat. Daniel Delis Hill (as quoted by FIT) says nearly every American man owned at least one matching sack suit by 1900. American jackets had squared fronts, British ones rounded. — [Sargent, Mr Stokes, 1897](https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1897-sargent-mr-stokes/)
- A blog post (citing McCord Museum, LACMA, an 1870 *West End Gazette* and Severa, *Dressed for the Photographer*, 1995) says the coat began in France as the "sacque" in the 1840s. It says the name describes the cut, not a sack-like fit, and that it had two back pieces where a frock coat had four. Illustrated examples run from c.1863 to c.1911. — [A Brief View of Men's Clothing: The Sack Coat](https://lilyabsinthe.com/category/mens-clothing/page/2/)
- Wikipedia: the modern lounge suit appeared in the late 19th century as informal wear for sport, country and seaside. Its jacket descends from the paletot (loose, no waist seam). The sack suit is the cut of lounge suit that became popular in North America: loose, unfitted apart from the shoulders, no darts. — [History of suits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_suits)
- Brooks Brothers' name for the thing is the "No. 1 Sack Suit". Two secondary sources date the name/product to 1901: Die, Workwear! says Brooks adopted the term "sack suit" in 1901 for it, and Bonne Gueule says "the birth of the No. 1 Sack Suit from Brooks Brothers in 1901". — [Die, Workwear!](https://dieworkwear.com/?p=30); [Bonne Gueule, part 1](https://bonnegueule.fr/en/blogs/medias/vetements-mythiques-etats-unis-tailoring-americain-histoire)
- Brooks Brothers' own 200th-anniversary page gives no year for the sack suit. It says only that the Number One Sack Suit defined American style for the first half of the 20th century. The same page dates the button-down Oxford to 1900. — [200 Years of American Style](https://www.brooksbrothers.com/200th-Anniversary/200th-Anniversary,default,pg.html)
- Die, Workwear! says the suit was Brooks's first mass-produced menswear item, worn first by store clerks before reaching boardrooms, and that the simple two-panel back suited mass production. — [Die, Workwear!](https://dieworkwear.com/?p=30); [Bonne Gueule](https://bonnegueule.fr/en/blogs/medias/vetements-mythiques-etats-unis-tailoring-americain-histoire)
- The Ivy sack: single-breasted 3-to-2 front (top button sewn under the lapel roll), no front darts, single hooked vent, less structured boxier cut, natural shoulder. Trousers lower-rise, belted, unpleated, uncuffed. Ivy style evolved on campuses c.1920s-40s and peaked in the late 1950s; Brooks and J. Press were major sellers; a 1957-58 figure puts about 70% of suits sold as Ivy style. — [Wikipedia, Ivy League (clothing), via Sack suit redirect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_suit)

## Disagreements and myths
- Date: existing entries say 1895 (brooks-brothers.md, edwardian.md). I found no source for 1895. Two secondary sources say 1901; Brooks's own page gives no year. Recommend changing the relation year to 1901, attributed as "Brooks's name for it, c.1901", or dropping the year. 1895 may be a conflation I could not trace.
- "Introduced": Brooks did not invent the sack coat. It existed from the 1840s-60s (FIT, lilyabsinthe). Brooks branded and mass-produced a plain version. The existing line "In 1895 came the unstructured sack-suit" should be reworded.
- "Unstructured": the 19th-century and 1901 sack was loosely cut; "natural shoulder" and the 3-roll-2 are attached to the 1920s-50s Ivy version. Wikipedia's Ivy page cites a Sears catalogue for Brooks's "natural shoulder" look. The 3-roll-2 origin story (Princeton students in the 1920s rolling lapels to hide the third button) is unverified (Bonne Gueule says so itself).
- Name: not "sack-like fit"; refers to the straight two-panel back (lilyabsinthe; Die, Workwear!).
- Source quality caveat: Bonne Gueule and Die, Workwear! are blogs; no book or museum source for 1901 found. FIT's page names the sitter "Newton Phelps Stokes"; the Met titles the painting Mr. and Mrs. I. N. Phelps Stokes.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Plate (front view): single-breasted, three buttons with the top one rolled under the lapel (3-roll-2), notched lapel of medium width, soft natural shoulder with no pad, no darts so the body hangs straight from chest to hem, low armholes, straight cut.
- Flap pockets (two hip, optionally a welted breast pocket), single hooked vent at back centre (not visible from front, note for hotspot text).
- Back: single centre seam, two panels (a hallmark per Die, Workwear!).
- Trousers: straight, plain front (no pleats), lower rise, belt loops, uncuffed per Wikipedia. Period 1890s versions had creases and cuffs per FIT, so say which era.
- Early version (1890s): often a three-piece with waistcoat, higher button stance, often buttoned only at the top; four buttonholes common (lilyabsinthe).
