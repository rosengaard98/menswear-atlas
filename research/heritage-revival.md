# Heritage revival (style)

Status note: loosely defined trend. Sources name it differently ("heritage boom", "heritage menswear movement", "workwear boom", "lumberjack look", "neo-trad", "Take Ivy 2.0") and none gives a clean start or end year. Dates below are each source's own.

## Claims
- W. David Marx (writing in Tokyo for The Business of Fashion) says Thom Browne started a re-evaluation of American trad style in 2004 with the charcoal grey suit, white oxford shirt and rep tie, and the wider industry then added British suits, tweeds, plaids and rugged workwear. This is Marx's account, not a neutral dating. — [An inflection point in menswear](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/luxury/an-inflection-point-in-menswear)
- The same piece says Japanese magazines *Brutus* and *Popeye*, with select shops Beams Plus and United Arrows' Beauty & Youth, promoted Anglo-American "neo-trad" for about two years. — same BoF page
- The same piece (undated on the page; refers to London and Milan menswear shows) says signs suggest the "heritage boom may finally be peaking": beards, Barbour coats and Indy boots losing novelty. It cites Tyler Brulé in the FT declaring the workwear era over and predicting well-tailored clothes. It does not call the trend dead, and says the replacement (darker, tailored, European) lacked a clear narrative. — same BoF page
- Marx, in a Put This On interview about *Ametora*, says Japan documented American style over seventy years, and that Big John and Studio D'Artisan pushed suppliers to keep making selvedge denim after US mills stopped narrow shuttle looms in the early 1980s. He also says Japanese demand for brands such as Alden helped keep heritage firms financially stable, and that Americans "have gone back" to Japanese examples of American style. The page gave no clear date in my fetch (context suggests around the book's release). — [Interview with W. David Marx, author of Ametora](https://putthison.com/?p=5438)
- *Ametora: How Japan Saved American Style* by W. David Marx, Basic Books, on sale 1 December 2015. Publisher blurb: Japanese consumers kept American clothing traditions alive when they had fallen from favour in the US; Marx traces about 150 years of exchange. — [Ametora, Hachette Book Group](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/w-david-marx/ametora/9780465073870/)
- *Take Ivy* (Japanese original 1965) was published in English for the first time by powerHouse Books on 31 August 2010, with a new English translation. — [Take Ivy, Simon & Schuster (powerHouse distributor)](https://www.simonandschuster.net/books/Take-Ivy/Teruyoshi-Hayashida/9781576875506)
- Self Edge (raw denim retailer importing Japanese labels such as Dry Bones, Strike Gold, Real Japan Blues, Flat Head, Iron Heart) was founded by Kiya and Demitra Babzani and opened in 2006, per a Q&A reprinted by Denimhunters (published 13 Dec 2019, updated 25 Oct 2022; originally for *Sportswear International*). Self Edge says it was the first retailer outside Japan to stock several of those labels (the shop's own claim). — [The Q&A I used to write about Self Edge, Denimhunters](https://denimhunters.com/?p=100789)
- A Self Edge Los Angeles shop on La Brea was announced as opening 1 September 2010; the article sells Japanese selvedge as "autumnal Americana". — [Self Edge on the edge, UrbanDaddy, 31 Aug 2010](https://www.urbandaddy.com/articles/11150/los-angeles/self-edge-on-the-edge-all-american-denim-via-japan)
- Charles Mcfarlane (2020) describes the heritage menswear movement as growing out of the 2009 recession, with heritage brands, hardwearing fabrics and vintage Americana influenced by *Mad Men* and midcentury design. He says that by 2012 it was already "the twilight" of the movement, and J.Crew (Men's Shop, Wallace & Barnes) was a major part of it, mixing workwear and suiting with raw denim. He blames J.Crew's decline on slipping quality, thinner trendier pieces and heavy discounting. Personal retrospective by a former shop employee. — [J.Crew deserved better than its swift, unremarkable death, InsideHook, 12 May 2020](https://www.insidehook.com/article/style/death-j-crew-mens-shop)
- Ryan Chang (19 Feb 2020) says American men of the 2000s consumed clothing in a way resembling Japan in 1964, and places the heritage craze around Rising Sun closing and J.Crew's Wallace & Barnes and Ralph Lauren's RRL moving from forums and blogs into the mainstream. He says heritage had been "dead for a few years" by 2020, and gives as reasons Americanisation through upmarket J.Crew offerings, Japanese brands moving away from period-correct looks, changing attention spans and the rise of streetwear. He gives no exact start or end year. — [Take Ivy 2.0 is the next great movement in American menswear, InsideHook](https://www.insidehook.com/style/defining-take-ivy-2-0-next-menswear-movement/amp)
- Victor Basa (Philstar, 18 Nov 2011) calls a lumberjack look (plaid/flannel, straight jeans, suspenders, beanies, sturdy boots, Red Wing as worn-in classic) a full trend, says it revives a 2006 "Mountain Man" look, and links it to nostalgia for "old world masculinity", economic mood and the Americana/folk revival (Bon Iver, Fleet Foxes). Luxury labels (CDG Junya Watanabe etc.) and H&M both sold it. A Philippine lifestyle column, not a menswear authority. — [Hard times: men rock the lumberjack look](https://www.philstar.com/lifestyle/ystyle/2011/11/18/749061/hard-times-men-rock-lumberjack-look)
- FashionBeans (page dated 16 Oct 2026, a retrospective list) mentions a "workwear boom" after hipster style, and credits online forums with making selvedge denim an obsession in the early 2010s, with straight-cut selvedge as the counter to skinny jeans. Thin, and the date looks like a page refresh. — [12 pieces of menswear that defined style in the 2010s](https://www.fashionbeans.com/article/menswear-defined-2010s)

## Disagreements and myths
- Start: 2004 (Marx, Thom Browne), 2006 (Self Edge opens; Philstar's "Mountain Man" look), 2009 recession (Mcfarlane), 2010 (Take Ivy reissue). No source gives one date.
- End: Marx's BoF piece says "peaking" (undated, I could not confirm its year); Mcfarlane says twilight by 2012; Chang says dead "for a few years" by 2020. The "2005-2015" window in the backlog is the atlas's own bracket and none of my sources states it.
- Causes: recession and nostalgia (Mcfarlane, Basa) vs Japanese archiving and supply (Marx) vs blogs/forums (Chang, FashionBeans). Mostly retrospective opinion.
- Marx's Japan-saved-America thesis is a book's argument; treat as his claim.
- Marx's own text says Americans' interest followed Japanese models; Chang says 2000s US consumption resembled Japan in 1964. These are analogies, not measured facts.
