# Lounge suit (garment)

## Claims
- The lounge suit grew out of the "lounging jacket" of the 1860s, cut to fit the waist with a long dart instead of a waist seam, and by the 1870s was worn with matching waistcoat and trousers for informal wear. This is the V&A's caption wording, as reproduced by a third-party page (I did not open the V&A caption itself). — [1800's, Historical Menswear](https://www.historicalmenswear.com/1800s/)
- The same caption says the lounge suit replaced the frock coat and morning coat in the early 20th century. — [1800's, Historical Menswear](https://www.historicalmenswear.com/1800s/)
- Advertisements used the word early: Samuels Brothers advertised "seaside and country lounging suits" in 1853, and Browne & Payne of Dublin called the lounge suit the leading style in 1859. — [The Importance of the Lounge Suit, Cathcart London](https://www.cathcartlondon.com/en-us/blogs/cathcart-london-journal/history-of-the-lounge-suit)
- Tailor & Cutter in 1880 described the favoured lounge style as a four- or five-button cutaway front with four flapped pockets, usually in Scottish check tweed. — [Cathcart London](https://www.cathcartlondon.com/en-us/blogs/cathcart-london-journal/history-of-the-lounge-suit)
- In the 1880s the Prince of Wales (later Edward VII) was reported wearing lounge suits. The article argues he was not setting a trend in 1907-08, since ordinary men had worn them for over fifty years. — [Cathcart London](https://www.cathcartlondon.com/en-us/blogs/cathcart-london-journal/history-of-the-lounge-suit)
- Late 1890s: "ditto" suits (one cloth throughout) were reported falling out of favour with the upper classes; 1905: flannel lounge suits reportedly outnumbered frock and morning coats in the West End. — [Cathcart London](https://www.cathcartlondon.com/en-us/blogs/cathcart-london-journal/history-of-the-lounge-suit)
- Jacket matching trousers is what set it apart: frock and morning coats were usually worn with contrasting trousers. "Dittos" was the colloquial name for the matching suit. Gazette says it developed in the 1850s-60s in Scotland (unreferenced, no tailor named). — [History of the Suit, Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/?p=121367)
- The Victorian frock coat split into two: the morning coat kept the tails, the lounge suit dropped them; in the Edwardian years frock and morning coats were mostly left to older men. — [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/?p=121367)
- Wikipedia: began in 19th-century Britain as sport and country clothing; emerged in the late 19th century; replaced the black frock coat as regular daywear in the early 20th century. Its history article says the jacket derived from the paletot and was shaped by darting the front panels (cites Waugh 1964), and gives "ditto suit" as a short-lived term. — [Suit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suit); [History of suits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_suits)
- American side: FIT's essay says the sack suit began around the 1840s-50s as leisure wear, spread across classes from the 1860s through ready-to-wear, and by the 1890s was a "serious rival" to frock and morning coats. It equates sack suit and British lounge suit. — [1897 Sargent, Mr. Stokes, Fashion History Timeline (FIT)](https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1897-sargent-mr-stokes/)
- A blog (Adam Lid) says the sack coat began in France as the "sacque" coat in the 1840s, with a two-piece back against the frock coat's four, loose fit, three or four buttonholes and often only the top button fastened; 1860s coats cut very loosely, narrower by 1870 trousers, closer fit in 1880s-1900s. Its origin claims are unreferenced. — [The Sack Coat, Lily Absinthe](https://lilyabsinthe.com/a-brief-view-of-mens-clothing-the-sack-coat/)

## Disagreements and myths
- Origin: Gazette says Scotland, 1850s-60s (unreferenced); Wikipedia says British sport and country wear; Lily Absinthe says France, 1840s (unreferenced); FIT says 1840s-50s sack suit. No source I read names an inventor.
- Dates of displacement: Cathcart gives 1905 for flannel lounge suits outnumbering frock/morning coats; FIT says "serious rival" by the 1890s; Wikipedia and the V&A caption say early 20th century. The atlas edwardian entry fits these.
- Edward VII: popular credit as fashion-setter is doubted by the Cathcart article. Wikipedia's legend about the undone bottom button is only "legend".
- Sack "name": the blog rejects the "like a sack" etymology and says it refers to cut; unreferenced.
- Beware name collision: the Met catalogue record titled "Lounge suit" (1981.12.4a-c, American, "late 1840s") describes a lounging robe in the banyan tradition, i.e. dressing-gown wear, not a street suit. It is not evidence for the daywear suit. — [The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/84622)
- V&A T.215 to C-1960 (Max Beerbohm's lounge suit) is dated 1918-20 on the V&A page I opened; the Historical Menswear page captions a Beerbohm suit as 1882. Do not use the 1882 date. — [V&A](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O13958/lounge-suit-unknown)

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- 1860s lounging jacket: waist-fitting, long dart rather than waist seam (V&A caption via third party).
- Sack coat: two-piece back (vs four on a frock coat), loose body, three or four buttonholes, usually top button only (Lily Absinthe, unreferenced).
- 1880: four or five buttons, cutaway front, four flapped pockets, Scottish check tweed. 1880s: lower fastening, edge stitching replaced bound edges. Late 1890s: longer, little waist suppression, rear vents return. 1902: single-breasted, small lapels, high-cut opening, two inside breast pockets, no vents. 1909: survey found most with a back seam opening into a single vent and two hip pockets (all Cathcart).
- Usually three-piece with matching waistcoat (Gazette); FIT's 1897 example: rounded fronts, notched lapels, deep V opening, high vest, trouser cuffs.
- The atlas sack-suit entry covers darts and the two-piece back; this entry should cross-link rather than repeat.
