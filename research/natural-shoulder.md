# Natural shoulder (detail)

## Claims
- The natural shoulder is a jacket shoulder with little or no structure: the shoulder seam follows the wearer's own slope, with minimal padding and no roping at the sleevehead. Ivy Style's lead writer calls it the defining characteristic of the Ivy look and sets it against "structured" shoulders (the military bearing of Gieves & Hawkes or Huntsman, the architectural roping of Cifonelli). — [The Natural Shoulder](https://www.ivy-style.com/the-natural-shoulder.html)
- Bonne Gueule lists "softer natural shoulders for comfort" among the features of the sack suit and quotes G. Bruce Boyer on the sack jacket having as little padding as a jacket can have while still looking structured. — [Mythical clothing from the United States, part 1/3](https://bonnegueule.fr/en/blogs/medias/vetements-mythiques-etats-unis-tailoring-americain-histoire)
- "Unpadded" is loose. A commenter on Ivy Style says "minimal padding" is more accurate, since a truly unpadded shoulder would hang like a shirt. The same page describes a modern Paul Stuart jacket as having a higher, smaller armhole than the Ivy sacks of the past (the designer's own claim). — [Natural Selection: Guess The Shoulder Maker](https://www.ivy-style.com/?p=4910)
- A measure used by enthusiasts: shoulder seam length, taken point to point from under the collar to the sleeve seam, of 6 1/8 inches on a size 40, scaling with size. Ivy Style reports J. Press shoulders were reportedly set by that figure for decades. A Southwick sportcoat measured 6 inches and a Martin Greenfield jacket 6 3/8, which looked boxier. The author says slope, padding and tailoring matter too. — [Six And One-Eighth: The Natural Shoulder's Golden Ratio](https://www.ivy-style.com/?p=4873)
- Ivy Style says the house tailor at J. Press, Felix Samelson, altered shoulders to get the classic natural look, and that the factory technicians who made natural-shouldered jackets are gone. — [Six And One-Eighth](https://www.ivy-style.com/?p=4873)
- FIT's Ivy Style exhibition page calls J. Press (founded 1902 by Jacobi Press, a Lithuanian immigrant) "the best quality maker of the natural shoulder suit". It gives no date for the shoulder itself. — [j. press blazers, FIT Ivy Style exhibition](https://sites.fitnyc.edu/depts/museum/Ivy_Style/exhibition/j-press.html)
- Mid-century Brooks Brothers: Ivy Style examined a Brooks two-button jacket and a No. 1 sack of the same period and owner. It describes the shoulder as natural, minimal padding with slight extension, a small high armhole and a generously draped sleevehead, giving ripples like an Italian spalla camicia. It says Brooks used this construction "for a brief period" and that by the late 1960s the natural shoulder had a smoother shape. This is one writer's reading of two garments. — [A Tale Of Two Suits: Brooks in the '50s & '60s](https://www.ivy-style.com/a-tale-of-two-suits-brooks-in-the-50s-60s.html)
- Southwick (the maker) is named in reader comments as the benchmark for the soft shoulder in the mid-to-late 1960s, with Norman Hilton second. These are comments, not the article's own claims. — [The Natural Shoulder](https://www.ivy-style.com/the-natural-shoulder.html)

## Disagreements and myths
- Natural vs spalla camicia: Permanent Style (30 Dec 2019) says spalla camicia describes how sleeve joins shoulder (sleeve runs under, as on a shirt) and does not necessarily mean a small, natural shoulder or gathered sleevehead; Simon Crompton adds in comments that shirring is a separate matter and a natural shoulder without shirring can be requested. A commenter on Ivy Style says spalla camicia is the Italian term for a natural shoulder. Treat them as overlapping, not synonyms. — [Video: What 'spalla camicia' really means](https://www.permanentstyle.com/?p=70530); [Natural Selection](https://www.ivy-style.com/?p=4910)
- Unpadded vs minimally padded: see above; sources use both.
- Origin: I found no source dating the natural shoulder's start or crediting an inventor. The sack coat is older than the Ivy look (see research/sack-suit.md). The hotspot note says Wikipedia cites a Sears catalogue for Brooks's "natural shoulder" look; I could not open Wikipedia (fetch blocked), so that is unverified here and should not be cited from this file.
- Whether mid-1950s Brooks had a Neapolitan-like shoulder is a commenter's speculation (hired Neapolitan tailors); the article does not say it.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Shoulder line follows the natural slope, fairly narrow, little or no pad (a thin wadding at most), no roll or lift at the sleevehead; the shoulder seam sits near the wearer's shoulder point. Seam length about 6 to 6 1/8 inches on a 40 per Ivy Style's enthusiasts.
- Small, high armhole; sleevehead eased in so it may show soft ripples (as in the 1950s Brooks example) or lie smooth (late 1960s per Ivy Style).
- Contrast for the drawing: structured or roped shoulders have a pad and a raised sleevehead that makes a visible ridge at the seam.
- Cut with canvas and sack-suit body (no darts), but the sources describe the shoulder alone; I have no source on canvas for this detail.
