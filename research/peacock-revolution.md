# Peacock revolution (era)

## Claims
- The "Peacock Revolution" was the name given to a 1960s revival of dandyism in men's dress, strongest in England and centred on London's "Swinging Sixties" scene. Abrams' blurb for *The Day of the Peacock* says the media gave it that name; the book's subtitle covers 1963-1973. — [Day of the Peacock, Abrams](https://www.abramsbooks.com/product/day-of-the-peacock_9781851776009/)
- Wikipedia credits the consumer psychologist Ernest Dichter with coining the term in 1965 (via a handbook citation), and George Frazier with popularising it in *Esquire* columns of 1968. I could not confirm either against a primary source; treat as Wikipedia's claim. Wikipedia gives a wide span, late 1950s to mid-1970s, with a peak around 1966-69 and a second glam-rock wave around 1972-74. — [Wikipedia, Peacock revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_revolution)
- Precursors: the late-1950s Mods took up Italian slim suits and short jackets; John Stephen opened his first Carnaby Street boutique in 1957 selling cheap, sharp, colourful suits; the Beatles popularised collarless jackets in 1963. — [V&A, The peacock revolution: 1960s menswear](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-peacock-revolution-1960s-menswear)
- Mid-1960s "modern dandy" look brought in frills and velvet, previously thought too feminine for men. Mick Jagger wore a Victorian guardsman's jacket on *Ready Steady Go!*, which sped up the military-uniform trend. — [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-peacock-revolution-1960s-menswear)
- Boutiques the V&A names as most influential: Hung On You and Granny Takes a Trip (King's Road), Blades (Dover Street). Abrams adds Mr Fish. — [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-peacock-revolution-1960s-menswear); [Abrams](https://www.abramsbooks.com/product/day-of-the-peacock_9781851776009/)
- Michael Fish (1940-2016) learned shirtmaking at Turnbull & Asser, made kipper ties by widening ties there, and opened Mr Fish on Clifford Street, Mayfair. Clients included Sean Connery, Mick Jagger (the white smock for the 1969 Hyde Park concert), David Bowie (floral satin "dress", 1971) and Muhammad Ali. The boutique closed in the mid-1970s: costly fabrics, generous credit, unpaid bills, backers leaving, then a fire. — [Culture Stories, Meet... Michael Fish](https://culturestories.substack.com/p/mr-fish-1960s-menswear-fashion-designer-london)
- V&A holds a double-breasted corduroy suit designed by Mr Fish and made by Hexter, c. 1968, plus Liberty paisley shirts and Fontenoy trousers retailed by Vince, c. 1967. — [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-peacock-revolution-1960s-menswear)
- Savile Row: new tailors settled in streets near the Row rather than on it. Nutters of Savile Row opened at 35a in 1969; Tommy Nutter mixed daring fashion traits into bespoke tailoring. Three Beatles wore Nutters suits on the *Abbey Road* cover; Mick and Bianca Jagger wore Nutters at their 1971 wedding. Apple Corps' building was on Savile Row (rooftop concert, January 1969). Hardy Amies opened his house at no. 14 in 1949 (women's couture first, later menswear ready-to-wear). — [London Museum, Savile Row](https://londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/savile-row-the-heart-of-london-tailoring)
- Late 1960s: Vietnam and the 1968 French student revolt turned some young people from novelty consumerism to second-hand and 1930s-40s vintage; 1970s lapels and trousers grew very wide, menswear and womenswear blurred, denim spread. — [V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-peacock-revolution-1960s-menswear)
- America: Wikipedia says the look arrived about 1964 with the British Invasion; John Stephen licensed Carnaby Street shops in US department stores, Sears opened mod shops, and the edge fed into hippie dress. Nehru jacket reached American menswear in 1966. Barneys of New York turned from discount to high-fashion men's retailer in 1968-69. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_revolution); [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/?p=104669); [VMSD](https://vmsd.com/thus-ends-revolution-scraps/amp/)
- Formalwear: coloured dinner jackets (After Six, 1965), Nehru jackets, ruffled shirts, velvet and brocade neo-Edwardian dinner jackets, flared trousers. — [Gentleman's Gazette](https://www.gentlemansgazette.com/?p=104669)
- Decline (Wikipedia's account): *Bonnie and Clyde* (1967) revived 1930s dress, which Ralph Lauren and Bill Blass built on; nostalgia from *The Godfather* (1972), *The Sting* (1973), *The Great Gatsby* (1974); John T. Molloy's *Dress for Success* (1975) marked the return to conservative business dress. The same page argues the impact was exaggerated and many men never adopted it. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_revolution)
- VMSD and Wikipedia both say the main impetus was British (Beatles, Mod, Carnaby Street), with JFK's looser, slimmer style sometimes credited as a precursor in America (VMSD says "historians" do so, unnamed). — [VMSD](https://vmsd.com/thus-ends-revolution-scraps/amp/)

## Disagreements and myths
- Start of Mr Fish: 1966 (V&A; Culture Stories) vs 1964 (Wikipedia). Wikipedia also says Fish started at Turnbull & Asser in 1962; Culture Stories places it in the 1960s without a year. Use 1966 for the shop.
- Who coined "Peacock Revolution": Wikipedia says Dichter 1965 and Frazier 1968; Abrams says only "the media"; VMSD says "they". Unresolved; say "the press called it".
- Period: Abrams 1963-1973; Wikipedia late 1950s to mid-1970s with peak 1966-69. Suggest era 1965-1979 is generous; sources support roughly 1963/65 to 1973/75. Check whether to end at 1975 (Dress for Success) so as not to over-extend.
- Wikipedia calls it "mostly UK" yet describes US retail; its own source argues the effect was exaggerated. Label as one view.
- Wikipedia's text has internal errors (century typos, "Fisher"); weigh it accordingly.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Slim-cut suits with wide lapels, high-waisted flares; double-breasted waistcoats and cloth-covered buttons (Christopher Gibbs, per Wikipedia only).
- Collarless Beatles-style jacket, Nehru jacket, Victorian guardsman's jacket, velvet and corduroy jackets, ruffled and cocktail-cuff shirts, floral and paisley (Liberty) prints, kipper ties (widened ties, up to ~5 in is common lore but no source opened gives a width), winklepicker and Cuban-heeled boots.
- Mr Fish corduroy double-breasted suit, made by Hexter c. 1968, is in the V&A (object details not opened beyond the article's list).
