# Ametora (style)

Framing note: Marx's book uses Ametora ("American traditional") for the whole story: Ivy, its Japanese rules, revivals, denim, outdoor wear, streetwear. A style entry titled Ametora is therefore broad. Recommendation: keep id `ametora`, period from about 1959 (Ishizu's Princeton trip, VAN's Ivy line) with to: null, and cover Japanese Ivy as its first phase. Miyuki-zoku (summer 1964) is a separate small topic; fold into ametora or van-jacket unless more sources turn up. I did not open sources on the heavy-duty / Popeye / denim phases, so those are only named, not claimed.

## Claims
- "Ametora" is Japanese slang for "American traditional". Marx's book covers denim, outdoor wear, rock 'n' roll and streetwear as well as Ivy, and is structured around Ivy's rise and fall and its revivals (1980s preppy, late-2000s neo-trad). — [W. David Marx on How Japan Saved American Trad](https://www.ivy-style.com/w-david-marx-on-how-japan-saved-american-trad.html)
- Marx says Japan kept Ivy knowledge alive while the look nearly vanished in the US, crediting VAN Jacket, Beams+, illustrator Kazuo Hozumi and the magazines Men's Club, Popeye and Free&Easy; and says Japan built many shops selling only American-style clothing. — same
- Kensuke Ishizu was born in Okayama in 1911 (Ivy Style says 1911, died 2005 aged 94; Lapham's says died 24 May 2005 aged 93). — [The Man Who Brought Ivy To Japan](https://www.ivy-style.com/the-man-who-brought-ivy-to-japan.html); [The Climb of Ivy](https://www.laphamsquarterly.org/node/5995)
- He worked in Tianjin before the war (Ivy Style: at a Western gentlemen's store; Lapham's: from 1939), met American soldiers after the war including a Princeton man, and ran Ishizu Shoten in Osaka. — Ivy Style; Lapham's
- VAN Jacket was founded in 1951 in Osaka (Minami-ku) by Ishizu. Name origin: Ivy Style says it came from the left-wing tabloid *Vanguard*; Lapham's says from a comic book title. — Ivy Style; [Van Jacket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Jacket); Lapham's
- VAN began with British-style suits and traditional menswear; from the late 1950s it made a full East Coast college line. — Ivy Style
- Ishizu visited the US in 1959 including Princeton, and launched an "Ivy model" suit that Lapham's says copied Brooks Brothers' Number One Sack Suit. Marx (Ametora interview) says he first doubted Ivy would work in Japan because the market favoured European fashion. — Lapham's; [Fader interview](https://www.thefader.com/2016/04/15/ametora-author-interview-japanese-american-style); Ivy Style (Marx interview)
- Toshiyuki Kurosu: first saw the word "Ivy" in 1954 in the first issue of *Otoko no Fukushoku* (later *Men's Club*); joined VAN in 1961 aged 24 and says he persuaded Ishizu to focus on Ivy. Early Japanese tailors did not understand Ivy cuts (a two-button jacket with a third button added). — [Talk Ivy: An Interview With Toshiyuki Kurosu](https://www.ivy-style.com/?p=3510)
- Lapham's gives a different order: Ishizu hired Kurosu as consultant in 1961; Kurosu and Hozumi founded the Traditional Ivy Leaguers Club, featured in 1959; Kurosu's "Ivy Leaguers on the Street" column began spring 1963, and his rules shaped Japanese fashion's reliance on rules. Kurosu himself compares localisation of Ivy to tonkatsu and says it had to be taught through "form" (katachi). — Lapham's; Kurosu interview
- VAN promoted items through magazine copy: T-shirts sold only after *Men's Club* declared all American students wear them. — Kurosu interview
- In 1963 Ishizu introduced the TPO (time, place, occasion) idea; his guidebook *When, Where, What to Wear* was a bestseller. — Lapham's
- *Heibon Punch* debuted 28 April 1964 with Ivy-dressed boys on the cover; first issue 620,000 copies, one million within two years (Lapham's). Ivy Style says only that 1964 *Heibon Punch* adopted Ivy as its look. — Lapham's; Ivy Style
- Miyuki-zoku: young people in Ivy clothes gathered on Miyuki Street in Ginza in summer 1964, buying at VAN's Teijin Men's Shop (Lapham's). Put This On, quoting Marx, estimates about 700 at the peak; Lapham's says about 2,000 each weekend. Clothes: button-down oxfords, madras, slim high-water khaki and white trousers, penny loafers, three-button jackets, seven-three hair parting; they changed out of school uniforms in cafe bathrooms and carried them in brown paper bags. — Lapham's; [The Miyuki-Zoku, 1964](https://putthison.com/?p=4622)
- Parents and PTAs pushed to ban Ivy clothing; some small-town schools barred VAN bags; the media called the Miyuki crowd a national embarrassment; a 16-year-old said they hated the label and were "Ivy". — Lapham's; Put This On (disapproval)
- Kurosu says the Miyuki-zoku "hurt" Ivy's image, which motivated *Take Ivy* to show real Ivy students dressing better. — Kurosu interview
- *Take Ivy* (1965): Kurosu says Ishizu probably proposed it, it began as a film, the team planned eight campuses but reached six around end of May when students were largely away; Dartmouth allowed filming. Kurosu says Ivy peaked in 1965 and felt like the past by 1966-67. — Kurosu interview
- Ivy Style says the title comes from Brubeck's "Take Five" (a claim from that article; I did not check it elsewhere). — Ivy Style
- Tokyo Olympics: the Japanese team wore red blazers with gold buttons, an idea Kurosu attributes to Ishizu; year not given on the page. — Kurosu interview
- VAN revenue: 3.6 billion yen in 1967, 6.9 billion by end of the decade (Lapham's); peak ¥45.2 billion in fiscal year to Feb 1975 (Wikipedia, citing Marx 2017).
- Collapse: VAN filed under the Corporate Reorganization Law on 6 April 1978; Van Jacket New Co. set up 3 Dec 1980, renamed 25 March 1981; bankrupt again 1984; Itochu bought/licensed it in 2000 (Wikipedia says bought; Ivy Style says licensed). — Van Jacket; Ivy Style
- 1980s revival: US preppy trend and a Japanese edition of *The Official Preppy Handbook*; in 1982 *Hot-Dog Press* devoted an issue to Ishizu, the first of that magazine to outsell *Popeye*. — Ivy Style; Van Jacket
- Tadashi Yanai's father ran a VAN franchise renamed Men's Shop OS; Yanai opened the first Uniqlo in Hiroshima in 1985. — Lapham's
- *Take Ivy* went viral after a 2008 US blog post; English edition 2010 (powerHouse), about 50,000 copies sold. — Van Jacket (citing The New Yorker, Marx 2015)

## Disagreements and myths
- Ishizu death age 94 (Ivy Style) vs 93 (Lapham's). Ishizu's Tianjin dates: pre-1943 vs from 1939.
- VAN name origin: *Vanguard* tabloid (Ivy Style) vs comic book title (Lapham's).
- Miyuki-zoku size: ~700 (Put This On/Marx) vs ~2,000 per weekend (Lapham's). Wikipedia says "Miyuki Avenue", others "Miyuki Street".
- Who turned VAN to Ivy: Kurosu says he persuaded Ishizu (his own account); Marx credits Ishizu's 1959 Princeton trip.
- Itochu 2000: bought (Wikipedia) vs licensed (Ivy Style).
- The 1964 Ginza police roundup, often told in popular accounts, was not in any page I opened; do not include.
- Kurosu's own remarks (peak 1965, persuasion) are a participant's recollection.

## Construction notes (garments and details)
- Ivy items as Japanese youth wore them 1964: button-down oxford, madras, slim cropped khaki/white trousers, penny loafers, three-button jacket, brown paper bag, seven-three hair part. Illustrator note: cropped trousers visible above loafers.
- Early mis-made versions: third button added to a two-button jacket; button-down cut like a long-sleeve Hawaiian shirt with two collar buttons (Kurosu).
- Gant button-downs from Paul Stuart were recommended to the Take Ivy team by US students.
